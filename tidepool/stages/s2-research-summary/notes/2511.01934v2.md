# 2511.01934v2 — Tool Zero: Training Tool-Augmented LLMs via Pure RL from Scratch

**Relevance:** high
Gives a concrete GRPO reward recipe (progressive overlap-reward → strict AST/schema-correctness reward) directly usable in this project's GRPO rung, a named reward-hacking failure mode, and a scale-down warning (1.5B underperforming 3B/7B) that bears directly on a ~1.2B target.

## TL;DR

The paper trains Qwen2.5 **base** models (1.5B/3B/7B/32B) with pure GRPO (no SFT at all) using a reward that starts as a lenient token-overlap score and smoothly transitions (via a sigmoid schedule) to a strict AST-match check on the tool call, plus small bonuses/penalties for multi-tool collaboration and parameter-value errors. This "Tool-Zero" approach beats SFT and SFT-then-RL baselines on BFCLv3 and four other function-calling benchmarks, and the paper argues SFT alone systematically overfits to training-distribution tool names/formats while RL generalizes better to unseen tools.

## Techniques to try

- Two-stage GRPO reward: early training uses a lenient set-overlap score over tokenized response vs. ground truth ("$r_{\text{general}}=-0.5+\frac{|\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{\ast}|}{|\mathcal{Y}^{\ast}|}\in[-0.5,+0.5]$"), later transitioning to a strict pass/fail AST-equivalence check ("$r_{\text{ast}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\text{AST}(y)\equiv\mathcal{T}^{\ast},\\ 0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$"), blended via a sigmoid schedule over training step — a template for combining "schema validity" and "execution correctness" verifiable rewards in the GRPO rung of our own ladder. (Section 4.2)
- Tool-specific reward shaping on top of the strict reward: "(1) multi-tool collaboration: $+0.3$ reward for reinforcing correct collaborative tool usage patterns in multi-step tool chains or dialogs. (2) parameter value error: $+0.3$ penalty per invalid parameter value" — a possible pattern for rewarding "flag rather than assert" behavior on malformed tool returns. (Section 4.2)
- Function/parameter name-masking during training data prep to fight overfitting to naming conventions: "we adopt a name-masking strategy aligned with lin2024hammer, which masks function names (e.g., calculate_sum → func_1) and parameter names (e.g., input_list → param_1)... reduces overfitting to superficial naming patterns" — relevant to this project's standing constraint of specializing to the underlying technology rather than one vendor's naming idiosyncrasies. (Section 4.1)
- Format reward as a simple binary gate separate from content correctness: "The format reward $\mathcal{R}_{\text{format}}\in\{0,1\}$ checks whether the model output contains all required special tokens in the correct order (i.e., <think>...</think><answer>...</answer>)." (Section 4.2)

## Baselines & datasets used

- Training data: ToolACE (general-purpose tool-use, ~97–102K single-turn + ~2K multi-turn) and xLAM (compositional, multi-tool-call, ~67.5K single-turn, augmented to ~10.3K multi-turn via four synthetic multi-turn augmentation strategies), plus an "irrelevance-augmented" xLAM subset. (Section 4.1, Table 2)
- Eval benchmarks: BFCL-v3 (Non-Live/Live/Multi-Turn), API-Bank, Nexus Raven, Tool-Alpaca, Seal-Tools. (Section 5.1, Appendix B)
- Baselines compared: vanilla Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct/Qwen2.5-7B/32B-Instruct, SFT models (ToolACE-8B, xLAM-7B-fc/r, Hammer2.1-7B), API-based closed models (GPT-3.5/4o, Gemini-2.0), and R1-like RL-with-SFT models (QwQ-32B, DeepSeek-R1, Tool-N1-7B/14B, ToolRL-7B).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- SFT overfits to in-distribution tool formats and fails to generalize: "SFT enhances in-distribution performance but weakens generalization to unseen scenarios." e.g. xLAM-7B-fc topped BFCL but had the lowest average across 5 benchmarks. (Section 3.1, Table 1)
- Reward hacking risk in the progressive-reward design itself: a hyperparameter sweep on the transition midpoint found "excessive exploration in fine-grained schemes may induce reward hacking and overfitting to superficial cues" when the transition to the strict reward happens too late (midpoint ≥ 50 vs. best at 25). Directly answers this project's open question about reward hacking when the reward is a schema/correctness check. (Section 5.3.1)
- Scale-down does not transfer cleanly: "The results, in Table 9, showed that the method performed strongly on 7B and 3B base models, while results on 1.5B and 32B models were relatively lower, indicating partial scalability and room for optimization in extreme-scale scenarios." Qwen2.5-1.5B-base + GG-GRPO scored avg 63.73 vs. 70.87 (3B), 77.32 (7B), 78.99 (32B) across BFCL-v3/API-Bank/SealTool/Tool-Alpaca/Nexus Raven (Table 9) — a warning sign for a ~1.2B target, since even the smallest tested scale (1.5B) already underperformed relative to 3B/7B.
- Starting from an instruction-tuned model hurt RL training in their setup: "training with the Base model consistently yields better performance across various training datasets compared to the Instruct model... models with stronger instruction-following capabilities do not necessarily bring greater training benefits to tool-augmented models in RL... higher plasticity [of the Base model] more easily elicits intrinsic reasoning abilities." This project's target checkpoint is `LFM2.5-1.2B-Instruct` (already instruction-tuned), so this finding may not transfer directly, but it flags a real risk that GRPO on an already-instruct-tuned small model could behave differently (possibly worse) than on a base model. (Section 5.3.2)
- Compute cost caveat: "The progressive reward-switching strategy... introduces additional computational costs during the RL training phase, particularly for large models... This limits scalability on resource-constrained hardware without further optimization." Reported cost: 7B/32B GRPO runs took ~28 hours on 5×8 Ascend 910B NPUs per run — a large-cluster reference class, not directly comparable to a single-GPU-scale training budget. (Section 7, Appendix A.1)

## Metric / eval choices

- BFCL assessed via "Abstract Syntax Tree Evaluation and Executable Function Evaluation Accuracy," other benchmarks (API-Bank, Nexus Raven, Tool-Alpaca, Seal-Tools) via "Function and Parameter matching F1 score." (Appendix B)
- Cross-dataset toolset overlap is reported as low as 0% (xLAM vs. API-Bank) up to 47.1% (ToolACE vs. SealTool), used to argue generalization must come from reasoning rather than memorized tool sets — useful context if this project wants to justify held-out/novel-tool eval design. (Appendix B, Table 8)

## Open questions for our project

- The paper's central negative result (Base model plasticity > Instruct model for GRPO tool-use training) is in tension with this project's starting point of an already-instruct-tuned `LFM2.5-1.2B-Instruct`; does GRPO on top of an instruct checkpoint still yield the generalization gains claimed here, or would we need to be more conservative (e.g., stick to LoRA/DPO) precisely because the plasticity advantage is gone?
- Given the paper's own scale-down result (1.5B underperforming 3B/7B), is GRPO with this reward design likely to be worth the engineering cost at 1.2B, or should it be deprioritized on the candidate ladder in favor of SFT/DPO for this project's model size?
- The AST/schema-equivalence reward here is for well-formed correct tool calls; the paper does not test a "malformed tool return → flag" scenario, so it does not directly answer whether this reward-shaping style would produce the 0.80 flag-rate / 0.10 false-flag targets this project needs.
