# 2411.02454v2 — Graph-based Confidence Calibration for Large Language Models

**Relevance:** low
Real topic overlap with "flag rather than assert" (confidence calibration to decide when to trust a response), but the method needs 30 sampled generations per question plus a trained auxiliary GNN, is validated only on open-ended QA semantic correctness with 7-8B models, and has no contact with tool-call/JSON malformation detection or single-pass on-device latency constraints.

## TL;DR

The paper trains a small GNN on a fully-connected "consistency graph" built from 30 sampled responses per question (edges = SBERT cosine similarity, node features = K-means cluster id only, no text features) to predict whether each response is correct, and shows this beats likelihood/verbalized/DeBERTa baselines on ECE and AUROC for Llama3-8B and Vicuna-7B on four open QA datasets. It's a calibration-of-correctness idea, not a structured-output or tool-call validity method, and it is expensive: multi-sample generation plus a separately trained model per deployment.

## Techniques to try

- Deliberately excluding semantic/text content from the node features (only cluster-membership one-hot) so the calibrator generalizes across domains: "We choose NOT to feed in the embedding vectors of responses to avoid the GNN's dependency on textual information. This helps the GNN to generalize to questions from different domains." (Section 3.1) — a transferable *design principle* (keep the flag-detector's features distributional/structural rather than content-based) even though the concrete graph/GNN machinery is not.
- ROUGE-based automatic correctness labeling checked against manual labels was found reliable ("the lowest accuracy is 0.89") — relevant if we need cheap auto-labels for our malformed-tool-return probe rather than hand-labeling all 300 cases.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Datasets: CoQA, TriviaQA, TruthfulQA, HotpotQA (open-domain QA, not tool-calling or structured output).
- Models: Llama3-8B and Vicuna-7B-v1.5 — both above our 1.2B target scale, so absolute numbers don't transfer.
- Baselines compared: length-normalized sequence likelihood, Platt scaling, GraphSpectral (+Iso/+Platt), SelfCheckGPT, Verbalized Uncertainty (and Verbalized %), APRICOT (fine-tuned DeBERTa on question-response pairs, +Iso/+Platt).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Core failure mode acknowledged by the authors: "there are instances where an LLM might be highly confident in an incorrect semantic response, resulting in a consistency graph similar to that of a correct answer. In such cases, our calibration model may not provide an accurate confidence estimation." — i.e., confident-but-wrong outputs defeat a self-consistency-based flag, which is exactly the failure mode our malformed-tool-return probe needs to catch (a model that asserts confidently on garbage input).
- Feeding raw sentence-embedding vectors (instead of just cluster id) as node features overfits: "We see clear overfitting issues when GNN uses semantic features: the validation quickly shoots up after the initial dip. We conclude that GNN using semantic features could not generalize to test data." (Appendix G.2)
- Using ROUGE-L similarity as the graph edge weight (instead of SBERT cosine similarity) degrades badly on longer-form answers: "For the dataset with long-form answers (e.g., TruthfulQA), the performance is much worse than using the clusterID feature" (Appendix G.3) — a caution against ROUGE-style string-overlap similarity for anything beyond short spans (would also apply to comparing structured/JSON outputs).
- Inference cost is nontrivial and scales with samples-per-question: "Generating 30 responses using the Llama3 and Vicuna models for 6000 questions from CoQA and TriviaQA data required up to 4 hours, with an average of approximately 2 seconds per question." (Appendix B) — this is on A100s for generation alone, before GNN training; a 30x-sampling approach is a poor fit for a single 8GB consumer GPU serving single-turn agent responses under a throughput floor.

## Metric / eval choices

- Three calibration metrics used throughout: Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Brier Score, and AUROC (discriminative ability) — standard trio, worth reusing if we need a calibration-style metric for confidence/flag scores, but note these measure probability calibration, not the binary flag/false-flag-rate framing our project's sub-criterion uses.
- Correctness labels obtained two ways (ROUGE-L threshold at τ=0.3, or an LLM-as-judge prompt) and cross-checked against a small manual-label subset — relevant precedent for validating our own malformed-tool-return labels before trusting them at scale.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper's flagging signal is "does this response semantically match many other sampled responses to the same question" — it has no analogue for "is this tool return/JSON malformed," which is a syntactic/schema property, not a semantic-agreement property. Would self-consistency sampling (at 1.2B, on-device, single-turn) even produce a useful disagreement signal for malformed-input detection, or is that a fundamentally different signal than what this paper measures?
- All results are shown at 7-8B scale; the paper gives no evidence on whether a small (1.2B) model's self-consistency samples are diverse/informative enough for a similar calibration signal, or whether small models are simply more erratic and self-consistency degenerates into noise.
- No feasible way to reconcile the paper's 30-samples-per-question inference cost with our project's single-pass, no-retry, throughput-constrained deployment target — would need a fundamentally cheaper calibration signal (e.g., single-pass token-probability heuristics) rather than this approach.
