# 2605.30537v1 — The Long-Term Effects of Data Selection in LLM Fine-Tuning

**Relevance:** medium
Speaks directly to one of the project's standing questions (keeping general ability from eroding while training up a narrow one) and proposes an evaluation discipline the guardrail-metric checks already resemble, but the experiments are on an 8B LoRA model over generic skill/domain sequences (not tool-calling), and its own limitations section flags that tool-use agents may behave differently.

## TL;DR

The paper shows that selecting fine-tuning data purely to maximize current-stage performance ("myopic selection") can quietly make a model harder to adapt later: it slows future learning, increases forgetting, and hurts out-of-distribution robustness, even when two selectors tie on immediate score. It proposes evaluating any data-selection or multi-stage training pipeline with a "two-level scorecard" — current-stage metrics plus long-horizon metrics (forgetting, worst-task score, OOD robustness) — rather than current-stage score alone.

## Techniques to try

- Track a "myopia gap" / two-level scorecard across the project's own multi-stage ladder (LoRA SFT → DPO → GRPO → quantization): "The second level contains long-horizon metrics: future adaptation AUC, forgetting, worst-task score, OOD robustness, and update concentration. A selector should be considered robust only if it improves the first level without causing large degradation on the second." (Section 5.1, "Practical recommendation")
- When building the SFT data-mix ablation, keep a small standing "future-proxy" evaluation set representing capabilities to preserve, rather than optimizing purely against the tool-calling objective: "the proxy could be a small standing evaluation suite that represents capabilities the developer wants to preserve: general instruction following, math, code, factuality, harmlessness, and domain robustness." (Section 6, "What kind of future proxy is realistic?") — this maps closely onto the project's guardrail suite (IFEval, IFBench, Multi-IF, MMLU-Pro, GPQA Diamond).
- Don't rely on embedding/diversity-based data selection alone to prevent capability erosion when narrowing the training mix toward tool-calling: "Diversity is necessary but not sufficient. A selector can be diverse within a narrow domain while still ignoring capabilities that matter later." (Section 6, "Why not solve the problem with diversity alone?")

## Baselines & datasets used

- Random, loss-based, gradient-based, diversity-based (farthest-first traversal), quality-based, and utility-diversity ("UDS", Zou et al. 2025a) selectors compared under equal token budget, plus a diagnostic "LHAS" baseline that adds coverage/future-proxy/anti-concentration terms to any utility score.
- "The main experiments use an 8B-class open LLM with LoRA fine-tuning... LoRA rank is set to 16." Task sequences: skill sequence (general instruction, math, code, reasoning, safety) built from OpenHermes/UltraChat, GSM8K/MathInstruct, CodeAlpaca, MMLU-style, TruthfulQA-style data; also a domain sequence (general/biomedical/legal/finance/scientific QA) and an interleaved sequence.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- "Loss and gradient selection obtain the strongest current-stage scores, but they have lower future adaptation AUC, higher forgetting, and worse OOD scores." (Section 5, Table 1: Gradient selection Current 62.4 vs Random 58.2, but Gradient Future AUC 0.52 / Forgetting 9.4 / OOD 46.5 vs Random's 0.61 / 4.8 / 54.0 — both at 8B, LoRA rank 16, 25% selection budget.)
- "Gradient selection requires approximately 1.35× as many selected tokens as LHAS to reach the same early-stage validation threshold on the next task." — a concrete cost of myopic selection on subsequent-stage adaptation.
- "Quality-based selection depends on reward models or LLM judges that can encode their own biases" (Section 6.2, Limitations) — relevant given this project's own constraint that no model-scored stored reward may be reused as a training signal.
- Explicit scope limitation acknowledged by the authors: "our focus is supervised fine-tuning and LoRA-style adaptation; the conclusions may differ under RLHF, pretraining, tool-use agents, or retrieval-augmented systems." (Section 6.2) — directly touches this project's domain (tool-use agents) but the authors say results may not transfer there.
- "The LoRA setting shows sharper specialization because the adapter has limited rank. Full fine-tuning reduces but does not eliminate the effect" — relevant to the project's open "LoRA or full fine-tuning at 1.2B?" question, though measured at 8B not 1.2B.

## Metric / eval choices

- Future adaptation AUC: "computed by checkpointing the model after stage $t$, training briefly on stage $t+1$, and integrating the resulting early validation curve" — a concrete recipe for measuring whether a training stage has made subsequent adaptation harder.
- Forgetting = "the mean drop from the best historical score on previous stages"; capability imbalance = "the standard deviation of normalized task scores"; OOD score = average over shifted/held-out evaluations.
- "Myopia gap" metric: mean absolute rank difference between a selector's immediate-utility ranking and its long-horizon ranking — a way to quantify whether a chosen data/training strategy looks good short-term but not long-term.

## Open questions for our project

- Does the myopic-selection effect (strong current-stage gain, weaker future adaptability) apply to the project's SFT → DPO → GRPO ladder, where "current stage" = tool-calling specialization and "future/preserved capability" = the guardrail suite? The paper's framework suggests measuring guardrail-metric trajectories after each stage transition, not just at the end.
- The paper's own limitations note that conclusions "may differ under RLHF... tool-use agents" — worth treating its quantitative findings (e.g. the 1.35× token-cost figure) as suggestive rather than transferable, since this project is exactly the tool-use-agent case it flags as untested.
- No instruction embedded in the paper text that reads as directed at an AI reader; nothing to flag under the untrusted-data rule.
