# 2603.16177v2 — The Finetuner's Fallacy: When to Pretrain with Your Finetuning Data

**Relevance:** medium
The core method (mixing domain data into pretraining) is not usable since we start from an already-released LFM2.5 checkpoint and cannot touch its pretraining, but the paper's forgetting/overfitting analysis and its findings on replay and small-dataset repetition directly bear on how to keep general ability from eroding during our finetuning-only pipeline.

## TL;DR

The paper shows that mixing a small domain dataset (1-5% of tokens, repeated 10-50x) into pretraining ("specialized pretraining", SPT) beats the standard pipeline of pretraining on general data then finetuning on the domain data alone (NPT→FT): SPT gets lower domain loss, forgets less general knowledge, and needs less compute or a smaller model to match NPT's performance. All experiments are at ≤3B parameters (OLMo-1B sandbox), so the scale is comparable to our 1.2B target. Since we cannot re-pretrain LFM2.5, the directly transferable content is the mechanism explanation (why finetuning-only overfits and forgets) and the negative result on replay.

## Techniques to try

- If forgetting during LoRA/full SFT turns out to be a problem, consider a brief "specialized continued pretraining" (SCPT) style warm-up on the released checkpoint — continuing training with a small domain mixture before doing the actual SFT/DPO — rather than jumping straight from base checkpoint to domain-only finetuning: "we find that *specialized continued pretraining* (SCPT), in which domain data is introduced at later stages of pretraining, may still be more effective than NPT" (§3.2). Note this was only tested by continuing pretraining of a model the authors already controlled (an OLMo-1B checkpoint at 180B tokens), not on a released instruct model, so its applicability to LFM2.5 is unproven.
- Mixing general-purpose data ("replay") into the SFT stage can reduce forgetting somewhat, but should not be expected to close the gap by itself: "10% replay helps NPT, but NPT $\to$ FT falls well short of SPT $\to$ FT with no replay" (§4).

## Baselines & datasets used

- OLMo-1B trained on Dolma as the pretraining sandbox, with three ~300M-token domain corpora: MusicPile (symbolic music/ABC notation), ChemPile (chemistry text), ProofPile (formal math proofs).
- Model scale comparisons at 300M/600M/1B/3B parameters (OLMo-1B architecture variants) — within our sub-8B relevance window.
- A separate controlled study on 160M-parameter LLaMA models pretrained on FineWeb2 English/Japanese plus JParaCrawl v3.0 parallel data, used to isolate the effect of pretrain/finetune distributional overlap.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Repeating a small domain dataset too aggressively causes overfitting even during pretraining, not just finetuning: "for smaller datasets (3M–30M tokens), the benefit diminishes and can become negative... even small mixture fractions (e.g., $\delta=0.1\%$) induce excessive repetition, leading to overfitting during pretraining and degraded post-finetuning performance" (§3.2). Relevant if our tool-call/schema domain data is small — more repetition is not free.
- Regardless of paradigm, domain test loss "begins to rise after roughly 5 epochs of finetuning" (§2.4) — a concrete reminder to keep early stopping on domain loss rather than assuming more epochs help.
- Replay of general data during finetuning is "not a substitute for early domain exposure" — even 20% replay does not close the gap to models that saw domain data earlier (§4, Figure 10 caption).
- Distribution-similarity metrics meant to predict how much specialization will help are unreliable across domains: "C2ST AUC flips from $r=+0.90$ on the Japanese sweep to $r=-0.98$ on cross-domain, meaning it predicts the opposite of what we observe" (§A5/E.3) — a caution against trusting embedding-based similarity scores to decide how far our tool-calling/JSON domain is from the base model's pretraining distribution.

## Metric / eval choices

- Defines *relative gain* $\mathcal{R}_{\text{gain}}(\delta) = 100\cdot(L^{\text{NPT}\to\text{FT}}-L^{\text{SPT}(\delta)\to\text{FT}})/L^{\text{NPT}\to\text{FT}}$ to quantify domain-loss improvement (§2.2) — a loss-based metric, not a task-accuracy metric like our BFCL/IFEval targets.
- Uses five distributional-overlap metrics (unigram/bigram/trigram JSD, MAUVE, C2ST) to try to predict which domains benefit most from SPT, finding only "the domain loss of NPT checkpoints after finetuning" reliably ranks domains correctly (§3.1.2) — i.e. you mostly have to just measure it empirically rather than predict it from an off-the-shelf similarity score.

## Open questions for our project

- Given we cannot re-pretrain LFM2.5-1.2B, is there a compute-cheap analogue of SPT/SCPT we could run under our "queued jobs on managed hardware" constraint (e.g., a short continued-training phase mixing tool-schema text with general instruction data before LoRA/DPO), or is this out of scope entirely?
- The paper's "finetuner's fallacy" argument is that finetuning-only needs a much larger model to match a domain-specialized smaller model — does this imply a ceiling on how much BFCL/structured-output gain LoRA SFT alone can realistically deliver at 1.2B, and should that push us faster up the candidate ladder (full SFT, DPO, GRPO)?
- No instructions addressed to the reader were found embedded in the paper text; it reads as a standard empirical/methods paper throughout.
