# 2410.21597v3 — Reducing the Scope of Language Models

**Relevance:** medium
Informs the choice of training signal for "flag rather than assert" behavior (SFT vs. DPO vs. representation-based methods) and warns about a probe/classifier approach failing at small model scale, but the task itself is topic-refusal scoping on 7-8B chat models, not tool-schema/malformed-return flagging on a 1.2B model, so nothing here settles a specific target number.

## TL;DR

The paper studies "scoping" LLMs — training a model to answer only in-domain queries and emit a rejection token/phrase for everything else — and compares system prompting, SFT, DPO, a representation probe classifier, and Circuit Breakers (CB) across Mistral-7B/Granite-7B/Llama-3.1-8B. It finds SFT wins when the reject-training data is diverse, CB wins when it isn't, layering SFT then CB often gets the best of both, and CB is notably harder to tune and more robust to adversarial jailbreak-style attacks than SFT/DPO.

## Techniques to try

- Construct DPO preference pairs for a refuse/flag behavior the same way this paper does: "DPO requires pairs of preference data, so for accept queries we provide the dataset completion as preferred, and the completion 'I cannot answer that.' as rejected. For reject queries we do the reverse, preferring 'I cannot answer that.' over the ground truth completion." (Section 3, Methods) — directly transferable pattern for building preference pairs where "flag this tool return as malformed" should be preferred over asserting a normal answer.
- Consider layering: run SFT first, then a representation-level regularizer afterward, rather than picking one method exclusively — "we propose to layer CB on top of SFT... often preserving the best performance" (Section 3, Methods, "SFT → CB").
- Their probe design for a two-stage classify-then-generate approach: drop the first token position (anomalous magnitude), mean-pool per-layer representations, normalize to unit norm, concatenate across layers, then a small 2-layer MLP (hidden dim 256) for binary classification (Section 3, "Two-stage on Probing Classifier").

## Baselines & datasets used

- Built from Super-NaturalInstructions (SNI) task categories: Sentiment Analysis, Toxic Language Detection, Summarization, Text Completion, Story Composition, Dialogue Generation, Program Execution, QA, GSM8k, Alpaca — used to define in-domain "accept" vs. out-of-domain "reject" sets (Table 1).
- Task-performance proxy: Rouge-L between generation and ground truth ("Accept Score"), accuracy for the two classification-style categories.
- Extra classifier baselines beyond their own probe: a fine-tuned GTE-1.5 embedding model with a linear head, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as both a prompted classifier and an LLM judge, and Llama-3.2-70B-Instruct as an LLM judge (Table 3, Appendix A.2) — "prompted solutions at the same scale as the final model (8B, Sys. and Judge) are substantially worse both ID and OOD than the Probe baseline," and even the 70B judge wasn't better OOD.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Probing degrades sharply at small model scale: "probing performs poorly at small scales, possibly due to the inability of a small probe to disentangle the representations well" — tested down to Llama-3.1-1B (Appendix A.3). Directly relevant as a caution against a probe/auxiliary-classifier route for a 1.2B model.
- Probe over-rejects on the very inputs it should accept: "Probing appears strong across the board, though it sometimes leads to overrejection on the Accept set, which is undesirable" (Section 4, Rejection set diversity).
- Circuit Breakers is unstable and expensive to tune: "it was much more difficult to tune CB than SFT... performance can fluctuate wildly based on very small step-count difference," and optimal target layers are model-specific (Section 5, Appendix A.6).
- CB degrades as the training set grows: "CB has issues as the raw number of training examples in the dataset scales, perhaps due to the difficulty of simultaneous orthogonalization of many different reject instances" (Section 4, Additional analysis / Appendix A.7).
- SFT and DPO only edit the tail of the context in representation space, which the authors argue creates "cracks" an attacker can exploit, whereas CB perturbs representations across the whole context and is more robust under adversarial/jailbreak-style prompting (Section 4, Representation Analysis; Appendix A.4). This is a warning that naive SFT-based "flag on malformed input" training may generalize poorly to unseen malformed-input patterns unless training data is deliberately diversified.
- One attack (base-64 encoding the query) bypassed all methods regardless of training approach (Appendix A.4).

## Metric / eval choices

- Rejection was detected with a cheap string-matching heuristic (checking the first 30 characters for words like "cannot", "unable", "sorry", "can't", etc.), tuned against manual judgment on ~90 sampled completions, rather than an LLM judge — they explicitly tried locally hosted judges up to Llama-3-70B-Instruct and found "such detection to have much poorer performance both in precision and recall than the [string] methods" (Section 3, "Detecting rejection"; Appendix B.2).
- For Circuit Breakers specifically, since it breaks generation into degenerate repeated tokens rather than emitting a refusal phrase, they added a second detector for "a repeated pattern of 4 or more strings within the response" (Appendix B.2) — a reminder that different training methods can require different detectors for the same nominal behavior, which matters if evaluating "flag" outputs that aren't a fixed string.

## Open questions for our project

- None of this is measured on tool-call/JSON-schema outputs or on a model near LFM2.5's 1.2B scale with the project's quantization target; it's all free-text refusal on 7-8B chat models, so the SFT-vs-DPO-vs-CB ranking may not transfer directly to "flag a malformed tool return."
- Given the reported probe failure at 1B scale, this is a reason for caution (not a settled answer) if the project considers an auxiliary classifier/probe as an alternative to a purely generative "flag" behavior.
- The paper's finding that SFT/DPO only reweight tail-of-context representations (making them more brittle to attack) raises an open question for us: how much diversity in malformed-tool-return training examples is needed before a straightforward SFT/DPO flagging signal generalizes to unseen malformed patterns, versus needing something like a Circuit-Breakers-style representation regularizer?
- No unusual embedded instructions were found in the paper text; it reads as a standard empirical study.
