# 2404.04689v1 — Multicalibration for Confidence Scoring in LLMs

**Relevance:** medium
The paper gives a concrete post-hoc calibration method for turning an LLM's own confidence signal into a reliable binary risk score, which bears on our flag-rate/false-flag-rate targets, but it is evaluated only on open-domain QA hallucination detection, not on tool calls or structured output, so it informs rather than settles the plan.

## TL;DR

The paper post-processes an initial LLM confidence score (e.g. softmax over "True"/"False", or inverse perplexity) with "multicalibration" — calibration enforced simultaneously across many overlapping groups of prompts (from embedding clusters or LLM self-annotation) — and shows this beats plain calibration on MSE/accuracy for hallucination-style QA scoring on 7B–13B models. Its main practical contribution is a set of fixes (upper/lower-set bins, linear-scaling patches, early stopping) that stop the original multicalibration algorithm from overfitting.

## Techniques to try

- Post-hoc multicalibration (their best variant, IGLB) as a way to turn a raw model confidence score into a calibrated flag/no-flag probability per subgroup rather than relying on one global threshold: "IGLB implements all of the modifications discussed in Section 3 to mitigate overfitting" and "IGLB and GCULR consistently out-perform across all datasets, respectively leading on MSE and accuracy" (Section 5.2).
- Group definitions via LLM self-annotation ("querying the LLM by asking it various yes-or-no questions about the prompt") as a cheap way to define subgroups (e.g. by tool/schema/SDK type) for group-conditional calibration checks, without needing explicit metadata (Abstract; Section 4.2 "Annotations").
- Overfitting remedies worth reusing on our own small held-out probe: conditioning on `f(x) ≤ p` / `f(x) ≥ p` "upper and lower sets" instead of exact level sets ("the sets $S_{p,g}^{\tau}$ are larger and hence reduce overfitting"), and early stopping "when the MSE on the validation set ceases to decrease" (Sections 3.1, 3.3).
- Using inverse perplexity or True/False-token softmax as a cheap initial confidence score derived purely from logits, no extra model calls needed for the True/False variant (Section 4.1) — compatible with the reference 8GB GPU deployment.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Scoring functions compared: True/False softmax score, inverse perplexity score, multiple-choice softmax score (Section 4.1).
- Calibration methods compared: uncalibrated, Histogram Binning (HB), Linear Scaling (LS), Group-Conditional Unbiased Logistic Regression (GCULR), Iterative Grouped Histogram Binning (IGHB), Iterative Grouped Linear Binning (IGLB) (Section 5.2, Table 2).
- Datasets: BigBench, MMLU, OpenBookQA, TruthfulQA, MathQA, TriviaQA (Section 5.1).
- Models: StableBeluga-13B, Flan-T5-base, Bloomz-7b1, Mistral-7B-v0.1 (Section 5.1) — Bloomz-7b1, Flan-T5-base and Mistral-7B-v0.1 are at or below 8B, closer in scale to our 1.2B target than StableBeluga-13B.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Plain multicalibration overfits in practice: "The results also confirm that IGHB, in its original form, is prone to overfitting" (Section 5.2), motivating all of Section 3's fixes.
- Linear scaling alone, without also switching to upper/lower-set bins, makes things worse, not better: the $\text{IGHB}^{\text{LS}}$ ablation "results in poorer performance than IGHB alone. This observation is unsurprising, as within standard level set bins, the model remains almost constant, and linear scaling patches merely amplify the risk of overfitting" (Appendix J).
- Small calibration datasets favor the simpler method: "HB achieves the best results only on OpenBookQA, possibly because of the small dataset size which causes the more complex models to overfit" (Section 5.2) — directly relevant since our malformed-tool-return probe set is only ~300 examples, in the same small-sample regime.
- Explicit scope limit stated by the authors: "the provable calibration guarantees are designed to hold on prompts that are distributed as those in our calibration set are, and do not hold for adversarially generated prompts" (Impact Statement) — calibration guarantees would not obviously transfer to adversarially malformed tool returns.

## Metric / eval choices

- Average Squared Calibration Error (ASCE) and its group-conditional version (gASCE), defined as squared bias within (group-conditioned) level sets, used to measure calibration quality directly rather than proxying it through accuracy (Definitions 2.2, 2.8).
- MSE/Brier score used alongside calibration error, with a bias-variance decomposition (Proposition 2.4) showing MSE conflates calibration error and irreducible label variance — a caution against using MSE alone as a calibration metric.
- Binary classification accuracy computed at a fixed 0.5 threshold on the calibrated score, justified because "this is the threshold that maximizes classification accuracy whenever the scoring function is calibrated" (Section 5.2) — a reminder that our flag/false-flag thresholding is only principled once the underlying score is actually calibrated.

## Open questions for our project

- Would group-conditional multicalibration (groups = tool/schema/SDK type, or LLM self-annotated categories) actually raise flag rate at fixed false-flag rate on the ~300-example malformed-tool-return probe, or would the ~300-example scale reproduce the OpenBookQA overfitting pattern the paper itself flags?
- The paper never touches tool calling, JSON/structured output, or execution correctness — all groups and scores are for open-domain QA hallucination; whether a "malformed tool return" score behaves like the True/False or perplexity scores studied here is untested.
- No instructions-to-the-reader or prompt-injection-style content was found in the paper; nothing to flag under the untrusted-data rule.
