# 2408.12263v1 — Toward the Evaluation of Large Language Models Considering Score Variance across Instruction Templates

**Relevance:** low
Real topical overlap (LLM evaluation methodology and instruction-template sensitivity) but the tasks (CoLA, STS-B, MNLI, SQuAD, CommonsenseQA classification/regression), models (Llama-2/StableLM/PLaMO, 7B-13B, no quantization), and metric target (a variance-penalized aggregate score) don't map onto this project's fixed benchmark suite (BFCLv3, IFEval, Multi-IF, MMLU-Pro) or its tool-calling/schema-validity focus.

## TL;DR

The paper builds English/Japanese NLU benchmark datasets with multiple FLAN-style instruction templates per task and shows that a model's score can swing dramatically just from rephrasing the prompt template, so single-template evaluation is unreliable. It proposes the "Sharpe score" (mean score divided by a variance-scaled denominator, borrowed from finance) to reward models that are both accurate and stable across templates, and finds constrained decoding (regex-based) reduces format-related variance in zero-shot settings.

## Techniques to try

- Evaluate with several paraphrased instruction/prompt templates per task rather than one, and report variance alongside the mean, since "evaluation designed for specific prompts is inappropriate for instruction tuning, which aims to perform well with any prompt" (Abstract) — could be applied to how we phrase tool-calling/system prompts when probing IFEval/Multi-IF-style robustness.
- Use regex-constrained decoding to remove output-format noise from zero-shot evaluation: "constrained decoding with regular expressions generally achieves higher performance than greedy decoding" in the zero-shot setting (Section 4.3) — relevant if we ever score first-attempt schema validity with a fallback parser rather than raw string match, though our sub-criterion is explicitly "no constrained decoding or grammar enforcement," so this technique is inapplicable to that metric.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Five English NLU tasks (CoLA, STS-B, MNLI, SQuAD, CommonsenseQA) paired with Japanese equivalents (JCoLA, JSTS, JNLI, JSQuAD, JCommonsenseQA) built from JGLUE, each with 8-18 instruction templates per task (Table 1).
- 15 LLMs spanning Japanese-only, English-only, English+Japanese, and continuously-trained bilingual models, 7B-13B scale (Table 2); no model in this study is at or below the ~1.2B scale relevant to our project.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- "the pre-trained-only model achieves better results than its instruction-tuned version of Llama2-13B-inst" on the English fine-tuning benchmark — instruction tuning did not guarantee better downstream scores (Section 4.2).
- Training on a single template does not transfer to other templates/answer formats at inference: "the performance often varies due to a mismatch between the trained template and the answer format expected at inference time... Evaluation using a single template should, therefore, be avoided" (Section 5.1) — a caution against evaluating our fine-tuned model's instruction-following with only one phrasing of a prompt.
- Constrained decoding helps zero-shot but "in the fine-tuning setting... greedy decoding generally achieves higher performance than constrained decoding" (Section 4.3) — constraining output format can hurt an already fine-tuned model relative to letting it generate freely.
- Continuous training on a new language can cause zero-shot regressions even while fine-tuned performance improves: "it may have forgotten how to utilize it, leading to drop in accuracy in the zero-shot setting" (Section 4.1, on ELYZA-Llama-2-7B-inst vs Llama-2-7B-inst) — a catastrophic-forgetting-style caution relevant to our concern about eroding general ability while training a narrow skill.

## Metric / eval choices

- Sharpe score: $Sharpe\ score=\frac{\mu_{score}}{\alpha\sigma_{score}+1}$, where $\alpha$ controls how much template-variance is penalized, defaulting to $\alpha=1.0$ (Section 5.2) — a general pattern (mean over templates, penalized by their spread) that could inform a "robustness-adjusted" score for our instruction-following/format-compliance metrics, but it is not a validated fit for BFCL-style task-completion scoring and was only demonstrated on classification/QA accuracy metrics.
- The paper explicitly excludes few-shot/in-context evaluation from its design, citing prior work that "the few-shot setting merely represents the exploration for optimal input prompts, capped by the performance of fine-tuning under the same number of examples" (Section 3) — not directly useful since our evaluation is zero-shot tool-calling, not few-shot NLU.

## Open questions for our project

- Does variance across paraphrased system-prompt/tool-schema phrasings matter for BFCLv3 or IFEval-style scoring at 1.2B scale the way it did here for classification templates at 7B-13B? The paper gives no evidence either way for tool-calling tasks or sub-2B models.
- No instruction or embedded directive addressed to the reader was found in the paper; it reads as a standard research paper throughout.
