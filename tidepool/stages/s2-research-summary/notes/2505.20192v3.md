# 2505.20192v3 — BalanceSFT: Improving LLM Function Calling with Balanced Training Signals and Data Hardness

**Relevance:** high
Directly targets SFT for tool-calling models, evaluates on BFCL (our exact pre-registered metric), reports a concrete catastrophic-forgetting result relevant to our "keep general ability from eroding" question, and its hard-data-mining method conflicts with our "no model-scored stored reward as training signal" constraint — all three are decision-relevant.

## TL;DR

BalanceSFT tackles two SFT failure modes for tool calling: CoT reasoning tokens numerically swamp the loss contribution of the actual function-call tokens, and training sets are short on hard/edge-case examples. It fixes both with (1) a learnable-alpha loss that reweights CoT-loss vs. call-loss, and (2) a self-evolving hard-example resampling loop scored by an ensemble of proprietary LLM judges. On Qwen2.5-Coder-7B and Llama-3.2-3B it beats plain SFT and (on multi-turn) beats GRPO on BFCLv3, while a plain-SFT ablation shows severe collateral damage to unrelated capability (coding pass@1) that the balanced method avoids.

## Techniques to try

- Learnable loss-balancing weight between reasoning and answer tokens instead of a fixed weighting or uniform per-token loss: "$L_{\text{SSB}}=\alpha\cdot L_{\text{think}}+(1-\alpha)\cdot L_{\text{result}}$" where "$\alpha$ is a learnable parameter with $\alpha\in[0,1]$ ... we set $\alpha$ as a trainable parameter to avoid extensive hyperparameter tuning" (Section 3.2) — cheap to add to any SFT loop that has CoT + tool-call segments, worth trying before hand-tuning a fixed weight.
- Iterative hard-example mining loop: split data into "qualified" (used for cold start) and "hard" (used for iterative self-evolution) via a Base Quality Check + Answer Check, then have the model itself re-attempt hard cases and retrain on newly-passing generations each round: "Hard Data Re-sampling (HDR) strategy that establishes a feedback loop to selectively generate new, high-quality complex data guided by model errors" (Abstract).
- Natural (unstructured) CoT outperformed a "prescribed reasoning strategy" CoT format for cold-start data: "we observe that Natural CoT consistently outperforms Strategy CoT. Therefore, we adopt Natural CoT for cold-start data generation" (Section 4.2) — relevant if we generate synthetic CoT+tool-call training data for LFM2.5.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Training data: xlam-function-calling-60k (60,000 samples, 3,673 APIs, 21 categories, single-turn) plus Open-Agentic-tool-use (30,000 samples, 129 APIs, 8 categories, multi-turn) (Section 4.1, Table 4).
- Baselines compared: plain SFT, GRPO (via the Verl RL library, hyperparameters in Appendix B: batch size 1024, lr 1e-6, KL coef 1e-3, 8 rollouts), plus closed- and open-source instruct models up to 1000B parameters.
- Base models actually fine-tuned (relevant scale): Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, and (Appendix C.4) Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 — all ≤8B, in range for our project.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Plain SFT on tool-call data caused severe collateral damage to an unrelated capability: "The SFT method shows dramatic drops in pass@1 scores on all the benchmarks, notably falling from 0.866 to 0.470 on HumanEval and from 0.823 to 0.445 on HumanEval+" (Qwen2.5-Coder-7B, Section 4.5) — direct evidence that naive tool-call SFT can wreck general/base capability, which is exactly our "guardrail metrics stay within 2.0 points" risk.
- BalanceSFT's own fix only partially closes that gap and is not zero-cost: "BalanceSFT-7B maintains strong performance across all benchmarks, with pass@1 scores only marginally lower (within 4%) compared to the base ... model" (Section 4.5) — i.e. even the balanced method still gives up a few points, it does not fully eliminate the trade-off.
- The HDR hard-data loop depends on a proprietary multi-judge ensemble as the quality signal: "Each candidate response ... is evaluated by an ensemble of LLM judges ... (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-4o, Claude-3.5 Sonnet) via majority voting" (Section 3.3), and the authors flag this themselves: "while HDR improves data hardness, it relies on an ensemble of LLM judges for quality control, which introduces computational cost and potential bias from the judge models" (Limitations). This is a stored external-model reward used to select/generate training data — it does not fit our constraint that "no model-scored stored reward may be reused as a training signal," so the method would need a non-LLM-judge substitute (e.g., execution-based/schema-based checks) before we could adopt HDR as-is.
- SSB's benefit is data-dependent: "the effectiveness of the SSB loss depends on the initial quality and structure of the CoT data, which may limit its applicability in domains where high-quality reasoning annotations are scarce" (Limitations) — relevant if we don't have clean CoT traces for our own tool-call data.
- A concrete data-quality bug worth checking for in our own pipelines: the xlam dataset had "an extra space appears between the opening bracket and the function name ('[ forecast_weather_api(…)')" that "causes the Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) parser to fail" (Appendix A.2) — a reminder that upstream tool-call datasets can silently contain unparseable examples that need a syntax/AST check before training.
- GRPO was not uniformly worse: "the GRPO method achieves a higher score in the Single-Turn task (84.60 vs. 84.00), but the BalanceSFT approach outperforms it significantly in the Multi-Turn task (47.00 vs. 40.75)" (7B model, Section 4.5) — GRPO retained an edge on the simpler subset.

## Metric / eval choices

- Primary metric is BFCLv3 accuracy, split into Single-Turn (Non-Live/Live) and Multi-Turn (Base/Miss-Function/Miss-Parameter/Long-Context) — the same benchmark family named in our pre-registered claim, and their per-category breakdown (Table 2, Table 7) is a useful template for our own BFCLv3 reporting granularity.
- They additionally track HumanEval/HumanEval+/MBPP/MBPP+ pass@1 specifically to detect catastrophic forgetting of coding ability from tool-call SFT (Section 4.5) — analogous in spirit to our guardrail metrics (MMLU-Pro, GPQA Diamond) that must stay within 2 points of base.
- Secondary benchmarks: ACEBench (single-turn/multi-turn/atom/similar-API/preference subsets) and APIBank (Level-1 Call, Level-2 Retrieve+Call), used to check generalization beyond BFCL (Section 4.1, Appendix C.3).
- BFCLv4 agentic subsets (Web Search, Memory) were tested zero-shot-transfer style even though no such data was in training (Appendix C.2) — a cheap generalization probe we could replicate.

## Open questions for our project

- Does the SSB token-count imbalance problem even apply to LFM2.5-1.2B's expected outputs? This paper's CoT segments average 350 tokens vs. 31 for the call (Table 1); if our target behavior emits little or no CoT before a tool call, the core imbalance this method fixes may not be present, and the technique might buy nothing at our target model/task shape.
- The HDR self-evolving loop needs a non-LLM-judge stand-in (e.g., schema/AST validity plus execution correctness) to satisfy our "no stored model reward reused as training signal" rule — worth scoping how much of the HDR gain (Table 5: +4.87 multi-turn from HDR alone) survives with a judge-free hard-example filter.
- No results at anywhere near 1.2B parameters or with a hybrid conv/attention architecture like LFM2.5; smallest tested backbone is Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, and even there gains are smaller than at 7B (Table 5), so extrapolating to 1.2B is uncertain.
- No quantization angle at all — the paper doesn't touch 4-bit/8-bit degradation, so it gives no evidence either way on whether this training method's gains survive our GGUF quantization step.
