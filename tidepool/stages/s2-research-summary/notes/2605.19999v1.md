# 2605.19999v1 — LLM Benchmark Datasets Should Be Contamination-Resistant

**Relevance:** skip
This is a position paper proposing a new benchmark-release format (publishing KV caches instead of plaintext) to resist pretraining contamination; it offers no technique, number, or pitfall relevant to post-training a 1.2B tool-calling model or its quantized deployment.

## TL;DR

The paper argues that public LLM benchmarks get absorbed into pretraining corpora and inflate scores, and proposes releasing benchmarks as "contamination-resistant datasets" (CRDs) — i.e. releasing a model's KV cache and penultimate hidden state for each question instead of the plaintext — exploiting the fact that Transformer inference needs only cached activations while training needs the full token sequence. It then sketches two ways to make such latent-encoded benchmarks usable across different model architectures (anchor-model subspace alignment, or shared relative-representation coordinates).

## Techniques to try

- None applicable. The paper's proposal (encode benchmark questions as an anchor model's KV cache + hidden state rather than plaintext) is an evaluation-infrastructure idea for the wider benchmarking community, not a training or fine-tuning technique this project could apply to LFM2.5-1.2B.

## Baselines & datasets used

- No experiments are run; the paper is a position/proposal paper. It cites contamination rates from prior work as motivation, e.g. "Ahuja et al. (2024) reveal that nearly all major LLMs exhibit significant data contamination of up to 91.8% across popular multilingual benchmarks" (Section 2.1), and "a clean mirror set of GSM8K ... can reduce accuracy by up to 13% on popular model families such as Mistral" (Section 1).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Relevant caution for any benchmark numbers this project reports (e.g. BFCLv3, IFEval, MMLU-Pro): "Brown et al. (2020) flagged over 90% of examples in some benchmarks as contaminated during the evaluation of GPT-3" and "audits of state-of-the-art systems reveal that even controlled releases like Llama 2 exhibit significant contamination in over 16% of the MMLU suite" (Section 2.1) — a reminder that public benchmark scores (including guardrail metrics this project targets, like MMLU-Pro/GPQA Diamond) may already be partly contaminated in ways not specific to this project's own training.
- The paper notes existing mitigations largely fail: "recent research has shown that modern LLMs often circumvent these transformations because they have already been trained on the obfuscated formats themselves" (Section 6.2, on lexical obfuscation), and decontamination/filtering "becomes increasingly inadequate at accurately identifying leakage instances" as corpora scale (Section 1).
