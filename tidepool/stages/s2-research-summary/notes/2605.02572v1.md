# 2605.02572v1 — On Training Large Language Models for Long-Horizon Tasks: An Empirical Study of Horizon Length

**Relevance:** medium
The paper's core subject (multi-turn long-horizon RL stability via horizon reduction) doesn't map to this project's single-agent-turn prediction unit, but its SFT-then-RL pipeline details, reward-hacking pitfall, and RL implementation gotchas are directly usable if the project reaches the GRPO stage of its candidate ladder.

## TL;DR

Using Qwen3-1.7B/4B on Sudoku, Rush Hour, and WebShop, the authors show that RL training instability in agentic tasks is driven by interaction *horizon length* itself (not task reasoning difficulty), and that reducing the effective horizon (macro actions or subgoal decomposition) stabilizes REINFORCE-style RL and improves generalization to longer, unseen horizons. Most findings concern multi-step environment interaction, which is not this project's single-turn prediction unit, but the paper's SFT-initialization and RL-infrastructure findings (reward hacking, tokenization mismatch) are transferable.

## Techniques to try

- SFT-before-RL as a reward-hacking guard: run SFT initialization before any RL/GRPO stage, since RL from scratch on a verifiable/format reward collapsed into degenerate reward hacking in their experiments ("agents converged to degenerate strategies that avoided penalties through syntactically valid but semantically meaningless actions" — §C.2, on Qwen3-1.7B/Sudoku).
- Use a conservative SFT learning rate to preserve exploration capacity for the subsequent RL stage: "we employ a conservative learning rate 5e-6 during SFT (with 4 epochs)... this approach allows the SFT initialization to provide stable behavioral priors while retaining sufficient exploration capacity for RL training" — §C.2.
- If a GRPO/REINFORCE stage is used, maintain a strict token-ID-in/token-ID-out pipeline rather than re-tokenizing detokenized strings between inference and training engines: "maintaining a strict Tokens-In/Tokens-Out workflow, in which the training pipeline consumes the exact token IDs produced by the inference engine, is critical for preventing policy collapse in multi-turn settings" — §C.3.
- Normalize advantages (batch normalization of reward components) rather than using raw rewards; their ablation shows this is not optional: raw-reward advantage dropped avg@4 from 96.0% to 44.4% (Table 6, §D.3, Qwen3-1.7B/Sudoku).

## Baselines & datasets used

- Base/policy model: Qwen3-1.7B (main), Qwen3-4B (scale-robustness check) — both at or below the project's relevant comparison range.
- Environments: Sudoku (9x9 grid, cell-fill actions), Rush Hour (sliding-block puzzle), WebShop (web-interaction benchmark) for cross-domain robustness.
- SFT data: expert trajectories distilled from Qwen3-32B (Sudoku, filtered for correctness then re-distilled via GPT-5-mini for concision) and GPT-5-mini directly (Rush Hour).
- RL codebase: rllm v0.2 on verl v0.5.0 backend, modified for token-level consistency; REINFORCE-style objective with sequence-level truncated + geometric-mean masked importance sampling (Seq-TIS + Geo-MIS); also validated with a GRPO-style group-normalized-advantage optimizer.
- Eval metric convention: pass@4 and avg@4 (4 sampled trajectories per instance, temperature 0.8).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- RL without SFT initialization reward-hacked rather than learning the task, on a small (1.7B) model with a hand-designed step/format reward — directly relevant to the project's "how is reward hacking avoided when the reward is a schema check" question, though the reward here (Sudoku validity) is a different kind of check than schema validity.
- RL-driven generalization was narrow: models generalized well to longer horizons using techniques seen in training, but "performance degrades sharply as the required techniques become more difficult, even when the horizon length remains within the training distribution" (§D.4) — i.e., "RL fine-tuning typically amplifies and recombines capabilities already present in the base model, rather than enabling qualitatively novel forms of reasoning beyond its pre-trained repertoire" (§D.4). Relevant caution for expecting RL/GRPO to instill genuinely new capabilities (e.g., "flag rather than assert") rather than just sharpening existing tendencies.
- Retokenization mismatch (string-based inference APIs vs. token-ID training) was found to act as "adversarial noise" causing severe RL optimization instability — a concrete infra pitfall if the project's own GRPO stage is built on a similar decoupled inference/training stack.
- Removing importance-sampling correction or advantage normalization (ablated individually) each degraded performance substantially (Table 6, §D.3), reinforcing that off-policy RL for LLMs needs careful IS/advantage co-design, not just a plausible-looking single technique.
- Model-scale robustness check: increasing capacity from 1.7B to 4B did *not* resolve the core training-collapse pattern under long horizon ("training collapse persists under the default horizon" at 4B, §4.3) — capacity alone doesn't fix these particular instabilities.

## Metric / eval choices

- pass@K / avg@K over K=4 sampled trajectories per instance (temperature 0.8) as the standard success-rate reporting convention.
- "Step accuracy" as a diagnostic metric isolating single-step correctness from multi-step execution ability, used to explain *why* horizon-reduced policies generalize better (§4.4, §D.4) — a useful diagnostic pattern (decompose end-task success into per-step accuracy) that could translate to diagnosing first-attempt schema validity failures.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper's task structure (multi-turn interaction with an environment over many steps) does not match this project's one-agent-turn prediction unit, so "horizon reduction," "macro actions," and "horizon generalization" — the paper's main contributions — have no direct analogue here; worth double-checking there isn't a hidden multi-turn framing (e.g., multi-turn tool-use chains) in BFCLv3 where these ideas could still apply.
- No content in this paper addresses quantization, GGUF, throughput, or vocabulary-size effects — none of the quantization-related open questions in the brief are touched.
- No instruction-like text directed at the reader was found in the paper; it reads as a standard empirical ML paper throughout.
