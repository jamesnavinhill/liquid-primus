# 2507.22543v1 — Pre-trained Models Perform the Best When Token Distributions Follow Zipf's Law

**Relevance:** low
The paper studies how to choose vocabulary size before pretraining a tokenizer from scratch (BERT/mBART, 72M–320M params), but this project uses a fixed pretrained model with a fixed 65,536-token vocabulary and never retrains a tokenizer, so the core method is not actionable here.

## TL;DR

The authors propose measuring how closely a BPE tokenizer's token rank-frequency distribution follows Zipf's law (via an R² goodness-of-fit score) and show that downstream task performance peaks at the vocabulary size where this Zipfian alignment is highest, across NLP (BERT/mBART), genomics (DNABERT-style), and chemistry (SMILES/ZINC20) domains. It's a vocabulary-size-selection heuristic for pretraining, not a fine-tuning or quantization technique.

## Techniques to try

- None directly applicable: the project does not pretrain a tokenizer or choose vocabulary size — LFM2.5-1.2B ships with a fixed 65,536-token vocabulary. The Zipf-alignment diagnostic could in principle be used post hoc to check whether the existing tokenizer is "well-fit" for the target domain (tool schemas, JSON, code), but the paper gives no method for acting on that finding short of retraining the tokenizer/model from scratch ("we design an iterative algorithm that gradually grows the vocabulary and monitors how well the resulting token distribution aligns with Zipf's law" — Section 3.3).

## Baselines & datasets used

- BERT-Medium (84M–124M params) pretrained on OpenWebText + BookCorpus, fine-tuned on GLUE (vocab sizes 2K–50K).
- mBART (177M–320M params) pretrained on WMT, fine-tuned on IWSLT translation (vocab sizes 2K–140K).
- DNABERT2-style BERT on genomics GUE tasks (vocab 500–10,000).
- BERT-based SMILES model pretrained on 5M ZINC20 compounds, fine-tuned on MoleculeNet (vocab 500–8,000).
- All results are at 72M–320M parameter scale, well below the project's 1.2B target and far from a decoder-only instruction/tool-calling model.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- "further increasing vocabulary size to 35,000 or 50,000 yields marginal or even slightly worse results" for BERT/GLUE — oversized vocabularies hurt, not just undersized ones (Section 5.1).
- The authors explicitly flag their own scale limitation: "Due to hardware limitations, we only conduct pre-training experiments on relatively small models... our findings may not fully generalize to state-of-the-art architectures with billions of parameters" (Section 7, Limitations).
- Prior tokenizer metrics (fertility, parity, compression ratio) were found by other cited work to "not reliably predict downstream task performance," which is part of this paper's motivation but also a caution against assuming any single tokenizer-quality proxy (including this one) transfers cleanly to a new setting like tool-call/JSON generation.

## Metric / eval choices

- Zipf alignment score = R² (coefficient of determination) of a least-squares linear fit to the log-log token rank-frequency plot, used as a stopping criterion for vocabulary growth (Section 3.3, 5.1).
- Downstream metrics are all standard NLU/generation ones (GLUE score, BLEU, classification accuracy, ROC-AUC) — none relate to structured-output validity, schema adherence, or tool-call correctness.

## Open questions for our project

- Is it worth computing a Zipf-alignment / R² diagnostic on LFM2.5's existing 65,536-token vocabulary over our tool-call/JSON/SQL-dialect domain corpus, purely as a descriptive check of whether the fixed tokenizer is a good fit for our specialization domain — knowing that no actionable lever (retraining the tokenizer) exists in our project's scope? Given the paper only validates this at ≤320M-parameter encoder models with from-scratch tokenizer training, it offers no evidence either way on whether a 65K vocab structurally handicaps tool-call/JSON tokenization versus a 128K-vocab model of similar size — that question remains open and unaddressed by this source.
