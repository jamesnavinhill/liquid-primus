# 2605.15220v1 — Always Learning, Always Mixing: Efficient and Simple Data Mixing All The Time

**Relevance:** medium
Directly relevant to the "data-mix ablation" rung of the candidate ladder and the "keep general ability from eroding" question, but the full algorithm (per-domain LoRA probes, interpolation regression, cvxpy solver) is more machinery than a single tool-use-vs-general split likely needs, and the closest matching experiment is on a 7B instruct model rather than 1.2B.

## TL;DR

OP-Mix trains a cheap LoRA adapter per data domain from the *current* model, linearly interpolates the adapters to simulate different old/new data mixture ratios without extra training runs, and fits a regularized regression to pick a mixture — applied identically across pretraining, continual midtraining, and continual instruction tuning. In a continual instruction-tuning setting that includes a "Tool Use" domain on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, OP-Mix on top of plain SFT matches a much more expensive self-distillation baseline while mitigating catastrophic forgetting of earlier domains.

## Techniques to try

- Estimate the old-data/new-data (e.g., general-instruction vs. tool-call) mixture ratio by training a small LoRA on each side from the current checkpoint, interpolating between them on a grid, and fitting a regression to pick the mixture — "OP-Mix trains a single low-rank adapter (LoRA, Hu et al. (2022)) per data domain directly from the current model, keeping the proxy model on-policy with the model being trained" (§3).
- For a two-domain (old vs. new) split specifically, they use a 9-point grid rather than full Dirichlet sampling and a KL-regularized objective pulling toward a uniform prior: "we replace Dirichlet sampling with a deterministic 9-point grid over the old/new axis at $\alpha_{\text{new}}\in\{0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9\}$" (Appendix A.1) with $\lambda=0.05$ KL regularization toward uniform (Table 3).
- To avoid overestimating how much has been forgotten, probes are not one-hot per domain: "the old- and new-domain LoRAs are trained with a *10/90* split (old probe mixes 10% of the new domain into the old mix; new probe mixes 10% of the old mix into the new domain) rather than one-hot specialization; we found this to prevent overestimation of forgetting while still being mathematically correct" (Appendix A.1).
- LoRA config used for the proxies at all three lifecycle stages: rank 16, scaling α=32, LoRA LR = 2× the base fine-tuning LR, 0 warmup steps (Table 3) — a concrete, cheap starting config if replicating the probe-and-mix idea.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Continual instruction tuning setup (closest analog to this project's SFT stage): Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct fine-tuned sequentially on Tool Use (4,046 examples) → Science (1,233 examples) → Medical (10,000 examples), one epoch per stage, evaluated with the SDFT accuracy metric of Shenfeld et al. (2026) (Appendix A.4).
- Compares against plain continual SFT (no mixing), 10%-old-data replay, full retraining from scratch each stage (skyline), and Self-Distillation Finetuning (SDFT, reverse-KL to a moving-average teacher).
- Pretraining/midtraining experiments use much smaller models (150M/300M/530M OLMo-ladder architectures on Algebraic Stack/ArXiv/c4/Reddit/StackExchange) — below the project's 1.2B scale but the algorithm and hyperparameters transfer conceptually.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- "Continual SFT suffers severe catastrophic forgetting" without any mixing correction (§4.1) — a concrete illustration of the forgetting risk the project's guardrail metrics (IFEval, MMLU-Pro, GPQA) are meant to catch.
- Merging trained LoRA adapters into the base model directly (instead of using LoRA purely as a cheap proxy, then doing a full retraining run on the fitted mixture) underperforms: "Although better than Continual SFT, LoRA-Merge is significantly worse than OP-Mix, indicating that there are benefits to using LoRA only as a proxy" (§4.1) — relevant caution if the project considers shipping a merged-LoRA model rather than running a final full/higher-rank SFT pass on the chosen mixture.
- Explicit scale and domain-count limits acknowledged by the authors: "Our experiments top out at 530M parameters for pretraining and midtraining and 7B for instruction tuning, leaving open how OP-Mix behaves at frontier scale (70B+ parameters). We also do not characterize how the LoRA proxy and model merging behave as the number of domains grows to 10 or 100" (§6, Limitations) — no result at or below the project's 1.2B scale, and the project's target model is 4x smaller than the smallest instruction-tuning test model here.

## Metric / eval choices

- Continual instruction tuning is scored by "mean accuracy across domains" using the SDFT accuracy metric from a cited prior paper, not a schema-validity or tool-call-execution metric — does not directly transfer to this project's BFCLv3/schema-validity/flag-rate metrics, only to the general question of how much a mixture ratio protects held-out capability.
- Pretraining/midtraining use perplexity and a 9-task zero-shot lm-eval-harness suite (ARC, BoolQ, HellaSwag, MMLU, etc.), again general-capability proxies rather than tool-use-specific ones.

## Open questions for our project

- Would a full OP-Mix pipeline (per-domain LoRA probes, interpolation grid, cvxpy log-linear regression fit) be worth the engineering cost for a project with essentially one mixture decision (tool-call/agentic data vs. general instruction data), or is a simpler fixed-ratio sweep (as the paper's own "10% replay" baseline) sufficient at this project's scale and domain count?
- The paper's own numbers suggest data mixing and training-objective changes (SFT vs. distillation) are "orthogonal axes of improvement" (§4.1) — worth checking whether a similar cheap mixture-ratio search could be layered onto this project's DPO/GRPO stages, not just the SFT stage.
- No instruction to the reader was found embedded in the paper; the text reads as a normal research paper throughout.
