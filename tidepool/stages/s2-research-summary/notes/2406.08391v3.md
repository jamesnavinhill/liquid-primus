# 2406.08391v3 — Large Language Models Must Be Taught to Know What They Don't Know

**Relevance:** high
Directly addresses the "flag rather than assert" question at its core — it shows prompting/verbalized-confidence is weak and that a small LoRA fine-tune on ~1000 graded (correct/incorrect) examples with a divergence regularizer teaches a model to know when it's wrong, which is structurally the same problem as flagging malformed tool returns.

## TL;DR

The paper shows that asking an LLM to state its own confidence (verbalized elicitation, true/false logit scoring, or sampling-based self-consistency) gives poor calibration on open-ended generation, and that a cheap LoRA fine-tune on a small labeled set of (question, answer, correct/incorrect) triples is what actually produces a reliable "I don't know" signal. The resulting uncertainty estimate generalizes across subject matter, question format, and even to unanswerable questions, and one model's fine-tuned uncertainty transfers to grading a *different* model's answers.

## Techniques to try

- Fine-tune with LoRA "through the model's features" rather than a frozen probe on top of last-layer activations — frozen features underperform, especially in open-ended settings: "frozen features are typically insufficient for uncertainty estimates that generalize effectively" (Section 2, Related Work) and "Probe is insufficient to generalize effectively from MC to OE, but training through the features of the model (LoRA + Prompt) does generalize effectively" (Section 6, Format).
- Add a divergence regularizer between the fine-tuned and base model's output distribution on the target sequence to prevent the calibration objective from degrading general capability — measured to cut ECE from 29.9% to 10.8% (mean over six base models, Table 1): "we introduce a regularization term to encourage low divergence between the prediction of the fine-tuned model and the base model" (Section 5, Training and regularization). They tried both KL directions and found plain forward/reverse KL insufficient, landing on Jensen-Shannon divergence applied only to the target-sequence logits (Appendix C.1).
- Pose the flag/no-flag decision as a two-token multiple-choice completion ("i"/"ii" for no/yes) fine-tuned with LoRA, rather than a numeric confidence head — this "LoRA + Prompt" variant was their best performer.
- As few as ~1000 labeled (input, label) pairs is enough to beat baselines, with diminishing returns after ~5000: "we find that having around 1000 labeled examples is enough to improve performance over simpler baselines... the marginal benefit of additional data points decreases after around 5000 examples" (Section 5, How much data do we need?) — directly bears on how large a malformed-tool-return training/probe set needs to be.
- Test generalization by deliberately holding a category out of the fine-tuning mix (they trained with almost no social-science MMLU questions yet the estimator still worked on that category) — a template for checking whether a flagging classifier trained mostly on one tool-schema family generalizes to unseen ones.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Base/instruct models: LLaMA-2 7B, LLaMA-2 13B, Mistral 7B (and chat/instruct variants) — all well above the project's 1.2B target, so absolute numbers don't transfer directly.
- Training mix: ~20,000 examples sampled across 16 QA/NLI benchmark datasets (ARC, BoolQ, CommonsenseQA, HellaSwag, MathQA, PIQA, SciQ, Winogrande, etc.), 2,000 held out for temperature scaling (Appendix C.2).
- Evaluation: standard MMLU (multiple-choice) plus a new open-ended MMLU variant they constructed, graded by GPT-3.5 Turbo as an auxiliary judge (validated against human grading and substring match, Appendix A.3); SelfAware dataset for unanswerable-question detection; LiveCodeBench LeetCode-easy subset for out-of-distribution generalization to coding.
- LoRA hyperparameters: rank 8, alpha 32, dropout 0.1, 8-bit base quantization during fine-tuning, AdamW lr 1e-4, cosine schedule, batch size 32, 10,000 steps with 1,000-step warmup (Appendix C.3).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Verbalized confidence ("Give ONLY the probability...") and true/false logit scoring are both weak in open-ended settings: "prompting methods typically give poorly calibrated uncertainties (measured by ECE) and their calibration does not improve out-of-the-box as the base model improves" (Section 4) — a caution against relying on prompted self-assessment for the malformed-tool-return flag.
- Perplexity/sequence likelihood is not a usable confidence signal for open-ended (non-multiple-choice) generation because probability mass is split across equivalent phrasings and penalized by length: "perplexity is generally not predictive of a language model's correctness in open-ended settings" (Section 4).
- Sampling-based self-consistency baselines (drawing K=10 samples and clustering) have "poor scaling" — O(K²) semantic-similarity comparisons per prediction — and degenerate to trivial confidence-1 under greedy decoding (Appendix B.1); worth ruling out as a runtime flagging mechanism for a resource-constrained on-device deployment.
- Ablation showing the estimator is not purely "correctness pattern-matching on question content": when trained on the same questions but with the true answers swapped for wrong ones, the fine-tuned model still performs "on par with a Probe model, but significantly worse than a model trained on the original sampled answers" (Section 6.1) — i.e., part of the signal genuinely depends on the actual generated answer, but part is topic-based prior, which is a subtlety worth remembering when interpreting a flag-rate/false-flag-rate result (a classifier can look reasonable while partly keying on surface topic rather than the actual malformed content).
- On the coding OOD transfer test (Appendix F), fine-tuned calibration improved selective prediction (AUROC) on two of three models but *degraded* ECE for the weaker two models (LLaMA-2-7B and Mistral-7B-Instruct) despite being trained with zero coding data — a concrete instance of a calibration method that transfers for ranking but not for absolute calibration.

## Metric / eval choices

- Two complementary metrics: expected calibration error (ECE, absolute calibration) and AUROC (selective prediction / discriminative ability) — the paper explicitly notes these can diverge (e.g., sampling baselines had "good calibration" but "very weak" discriminative performance, Section 5), which argues for the project's own paired flag-rate/false-flag-rate reporting rather than a single scalar.
- They use an LLM-as-judge (GPT-3.5 Turbo) to grade open-ended correctness against ground truth, validated to have higher agreement with human graders than exact substring match (Appendix A.3, Table 2) — a candidate approach for grading free-form tool-call/JSON outputs where exact match is too brittle, though note the brief's constraint that no model-scored signal may be reused as a *training* reward; here it's used only to construct offline labels, not as a live RL reward.

## Open questions for our project

- All results are on 7B–13B dense transformer models; nothing here validates whether 1000-example LoRA calibration-tuning holds up at 1.2B parameters or on a hybrid conv/attention architecture like LFM2.5 — this is an open transfer risk, not answered by the paper.
- The paper's task is factual QA correctness, not structured tool-call/JSON validity — the analogy to "flag a malformed tool return" is structural (binary correct/incorrect signal, small labeled set, LoRA) but untested for that domain; would need a project-specific pilot to confirm the same data efficiency holds.
- No quantization experiments at all — doesn't inform whether a calibration/flagging LoRA head survives 4-bit GGUF quantization, which is central to this project's deployment target.
- The finding that one model can calibrate/judge another model's answers (Section 6.1, Self-knowledge) raises a question worth testing here: could a larger judge model's LoRA-tuned "correctness sense" be transferred or distilled into the 1.2B target more cheaply than training the flagging signal from scratch on the small model itself?
