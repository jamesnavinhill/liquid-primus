# 2511.20849v1 — Length-MAX Tokenizer for Language Models

**Relevance:** low
The paper is a from-scratch tokenizer/pretraining method that the authors themselves say cannot be applied to a frozen checkpoint without vocabulary adaptation, which rules it out for a post-training-only project on `LiquidAI/LFM2.5-1.2B-Instruct`'s fixed 65,536-token vocabulary.

## TL;DR

Length-MAX is a new tokenizer that maximizes `freq(t) × |t|` instead of frequency alone, built via a greedy graph-partitioning approximation, and shown (on GPT-2 models trained from scratch at 124M–1.3B) to cut tokens-per-character 14–18% versus BPE, speed training and inference, and slightly improve downstream benchmarks. It is a pretraining-time tokenizer-construction method, not a post-training or inference-time technique, so it doesn't fit a project that starts from an already-tokenized, already-pretrained 1.2B model.

## Techniques to try

- None directly transferable. The method requires building a new vocabulary and retraining token embeddings from scratch; the paper is explicit that this is incompatible with existing checkpoints: "a third limitation concerns compatibility with existing pretrained models. Because token embeddings are learned during pretraining, Length-MAX cannot be applied to frozen checkpoints without vocabulary adaptation." (Section 6, Limitations and Future Work)

## Baselines & datasets used

- Trained/evaluated on FineWeb (10B/40B/150GB shards for 124M/355M/1.3B models) plus RefinedWeb-1TB for systems benchmarks; downstream evals include GLUE, LAMBADA, HellaSwag, BLiMP, StoryCloze, WSC273, SQuAD v1.1, PIQA, and TinyStories (GPT-4-rubric scored). All at GPT-2 scale (124M/355M/1.3B), i.e. well below the 1.2B target model's parameter count but a similar order of magnitude to it.
- Baselines: BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece, all retrained on the same corpus for fair comparison.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Not usable on frozen/pretrained checkpoints without vocabulary adaptation (quoted above) — directly relevant as a reason to rule this out rather than a technique to adopt.
- The paper itself cautions against reading its 64k/100k vocabulary results as scale-appropriate: "For our 124M model, the predicted optimal vocabulary size is approximately 32k; thus, our 64k-100k experiments primarily demonstrate the algorithmic scalability of Length-MAX ... rather than optimal configurations for this model scale." (Section 4.1, Vocabulary scaling) — a reminder that vocabulary-size claims don't automatically transfer across model scales, which bears on the project's open question about whether LFM2.5's 65K vocab is a handicap versus 128K-vocab peers, but this paper offers no data at that comparison point.
- All experiments are English-only and at ≤1.3B scale; the 7B numbers are an analytical FLOPs-based extrapolation, not measured ("A FLOPs-based scaling curve suggests similar efficiency gains at 7B parameters" — Introduction), so even within the tokenizer-design literature this paper is not strong evidence at the project's actual (1.2B, quantized, tool-calling) operating point.

## Metric / eval choices

- Tokens-per-character (TPC) as the core compression metric, plus GPU-hours/wall-clock/steps-to-fixed-validation-loss for training cost and tokens/s + latency for inference cost — a reasonable template if this project ever needed to characterize tokenization efficiency, but not something the project's BFCL/IFEval/structured-output metrics need.
- Vocabulary coverage and OOV rate under clean and noised (3% char-substitution) text, used as a robustness check — a pattern that could inform how to probe whether LFM2.5's existing 65K vocabulary handles domain-specific strings (SQL, JSON Schema, MCP identifiers) well, though this paper doesn't test tool-call or code/JSON text at all.

## Open questions for our project

- Nothing here answers the project's stated question "Does a 65K vocabulary handicap tool-call and JSON tokenization against the 128K-vocab models in the same size class?" — this paper only compares tokenizer *construction algorithms* at matched vocabulary sizes, never different vocabulary sizes' effect on structured/code text specifically.
- No instruction-like text embedded in the paper requiring disregard; it reads as a standard research paper throughout.
