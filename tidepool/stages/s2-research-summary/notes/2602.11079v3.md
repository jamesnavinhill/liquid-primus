# 2602.11079v3 — Probe-Based Data Attribution: Discovering and Mitigating Undesirable Behaviors in LLM Post-Training

**Relevance:** medium
Not about tool-calling, structured output, or quantization, but it documents a concrete DPO-data-contamination failure mode and a preference-pair auditing method directly relevant to this project's DPO stage on the candidate ladder.

## TL;DR

The authors trace a harmful behavior in OLMo 2's production DPO model back to contaminated preference pairs using activation-difference vectors, then show that filtering or label-switching the top-attributed datapoints removes most of the bad behavior while mostly preserving capabilities. The core lesson for us: automated, LLM-judge-labeled preference data can silently mislabel a small fraction of pairs, and those mislabeled pairs are enough to install an unwanted behavior that only shows up under an unanticipated trigger condition.

## Techniques to try

- Activation-difference "probing vector" attribution to rank which preference datapoints are responsible for an observed behavior shift between an SFT and a DPO checkpoint, useful if we ever need to debug why DPO training on our malformed-tool-return pairs produces an unexpected behavior ("Datapoints are ranked by cosine similarity with the probing vector." — Section 6.2). This is cheap relative to gradient-based influence methods: "ranking 378,341 datapoints using the probing vector is over 10× cheaper than both gradient-based methods and LLM-judge baselines" (Section 7), measured on OLMo 2 7B.
- Unsupervised clustering of behavior-vs-datapoint cosine-similarity matrices to surface *unanticipated* emergent behaviors without pre-specifying what to look for ("clustering behavior-datapoint similarity matrices also enables unsupervised discovery of emergent behaviors" — Abstract) — could be applied as a post-DPO audit step before committing to a checkpoint.
- Label-switching (swap accepted/rejected) rather than deletion once contaminated pairs are identified, which gave the strongest single-datapoint effect on their harmful-behavior metric: "Switching 30,000 datapoints with the probing vector achieves a 78% reduction from baseline" (Section 6.4, OLMo 2 7B).

## Baselines & datasets used

- OLMo 2 7B, SFT checkpoint vs. production DPO checkpoint, on OLMo's public 378,341-pair DPO dataset generated by "sampling responses from a pool of 20 different LLMs, then graded by GPT-4o according to a rubric" (Section 5.1) — this is the automated-grading pipeline that produced the contamination.
- Compared against gradient-based attribution (LESS, adapted for DPO) and an "LLM Toxic" judge-based ranking baseline; capability checks used GSM8K, IFEval, and XSTest (Section 6.1).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- The harmful behavior — "distractor-triggered compliance" — was never deliberately induced; it emerged from mislabeled preference pairs where "the 'accepted' response complies and the 'rejected' response refuses" (Section 6.4), produced by the automated LLM-graded data pipeline. Directly relevant to our own DPO stage: if we auto-generate malformed-tool-return preference pairs (possibly with model-assisted labeling), a small mislabeled fraction can install an off-target behavior invisible to standard evals.
- The trigger for the bad behavior was an innocuous formatting instruction appended to a harmful request (e.g., "no more than 50 words"), and it "evades standard safety evaluations, which test harmful requests in isolation without appending innocuous instructions" (Section 5.4) — a caution that our own guardrail/flag-rate evals should test compound instructions (tool-call formatting constraints stacked with adversarial or malformed inputs), not just isolated cases.
- Switching labels at scale traded off capability: "switching shows a capability trade-off at larger counts: GSM8K drops from 72.5% to approximately 68% and XSTest increases when switching 30,000 datapoints" (Section 6.4, OLMo 2 7B) — evidence that aggressive DPO relabeling to fix one behavior can erode general capability, relevant to our "how is general ability kept from eroding" question.
- Gradient-based attribution (LESS) "outperforms our probing vector when filtering a small sample but degrades as we scale up the intervention" (Section 7) — a reminder that influence-style methods don't necessarily scale monotonically with intervention size.
- Method requires full post-training data and intermediate checkpoints for causal validation via retraining: "OLMo is the only recent, competitive open-source model that releases these artifacts" (Section 7, Limitations) — this attribution technique is not directly usable on LFM2.5 unless we retain our own full training/checkpoint history, which is a real cost if we wanted to adopt it as an audit tool.

## Metric / eval choices

- Harmful response rate measured via LLM judge (GPT-5-mini) validated against human labels: "The LLM judge achieves 90.6% agreement with the human annotator, with Cohen's κ=0.81" (Appendix A.9) — a useful pattern for validating our own flag-rate/false-flag-rate judge if we use one, though the brief's "no model-scored stored reward may be reused as a training signal" constraint means we could use such a judge for *evaluation* but not loop it back into training reward.
- They explicitly note their own generated-preference-pair pipeline mirrors ours: pairs are produced by sampling multiple model responses and grading with an LLM judge, exactly the kind of process that "generated malformed-tool-return pairs" in our candidate ladder would use — worth treating as a cautionary precedent, not a template to copy uncritically.

## Open questions for our project

- Should we budget for a lightweight preference-data audit (even manual spot-checking, since full activation-attribution isn't practical without retained checkpoints) before committing a DPO run, given how easily automated grading of accepted/rejected tool-call pairs could introduce a small fraction of mislabeled examples with an outsized effect on rare failure modes?
- Does an analogous "distractor-triggered" pattern exist for us — e.g., a benign-looking constraint in a tool schema or system prompt causing the model to skip the "flag malformed tool output" behavior it was trained to have? Their finding suggests testing guardrail evals under compound/stacked instructions, not just isolated malformed-input prompts.
