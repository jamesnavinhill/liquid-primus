# 2407.04069v2 — A Systematic Survey and Critical Review on Evaluating Large Language Models: Challenges, Limitations, and Recommendations

**Relevance:** medium
A general methodology survey on LLM evaluation rigor (reproducibility/reliability/robustness), not about tool-calling, structured output, or quantization for our target model, but it supplies concrete evaluation-hygiene practices and a couple of directly on-topic pointers (a format-following benchmark, a quantization-confidence finding) worth building into how BFCL/IFEval numbers are measured and reported.

## TL;DR

This is a critical review of how LLM evaluations are usually done wrong: undocumented prompts/decoding params, single-prompt evaluation, unvalidated parsing scripts, biased LLM-as-judge setups, and benchmarks that don't reflect real capability coverage. For this project, its value is less "what technique to train with" and more "how to not fool yourself when reporting the pre-registered BFCL/IFEval/quantization numbers."

## Techniques to try

- Measure tokenizer vocabulary coverage of the actual target domain (here: JSON Schema, tool-call syntax, MCP payloads) the way the paper does for MMLU/MixEval, as a cheap diagnostic for whether LFM2.5's 65,536-token vocabulary is a handicap versus 128K-vocab peers: "we conducted a small-scale analysis for LLaMA-2 ... LLaMA-3 ... Mistral ... and Qwen2 ... Our findings indicate that these datasets cover a relatively small portion of the model's capabilities" (Section 3.3.1, Table 2).
- Validate the malformed-tool-return parsing/flagging pipeline against a human-checked subset before trusting it at scale, following the paper's hybrid approach: "Laskar et al. (2023a) proposed a hybrid approach combining parsing script-based automatic evaluation with human-in-the-loop ... Initially, the parsing script extracts answers from LLM-generated responses. If any issues arise, humans resolve them" (Section 3.2.3).
- If any LLM-as-judge scoring is used anywhere in the pipeline (not as a training reward, but for qualitative triage), use multiple models as a jury rather than one judge: "Verga et al. (2024) proposed a new technique that leveraged multiple LLMs as juries instead of using a single LLM as the judge. This approach demonstrates higher correlations with humans, while mitigating biases" (Section 3.2.3).

## Baselines & datasets used

- Not applicable — this is a survey/critical-review paper with no new model training; its own "experiments" are small illustrative analyses (SAMSum prompt-variation study on GPT-4o/GPT-3.5/Claude-3-Opus; tokenizer vocabulary-coverage table on LLaMA-2/LLaMA-3/Mistral/Qwen2 over MMLU/MMLU-Pro/MixEval).
- Points to the FOFO benchmark as the relevant format-following eval to know about: "The recently proposed FOFO benchmark Xia et al. (2024) measures language models' ability to adhere to the requested formats in prompts across different domains" (Appendix A.1) — worth checking as an auxiliary structured-output eval alongside BFCL.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Quantization can quietly erode confidence even when a downstream ranking looks unchanged: "Huang et al. (2024b) found that quantized LLaMA-3 versions have lower output confidence than the original. They noted similar model rankings for perplexity and a common-sense QA dataset" (Section 3.3.3) — relevant caution for the project's own 4-bit GGUF retention target, though the paper only reports this at LLaMA-3 scale, not on structured/tool-call outputs specifically, and gives no numbers.
- Single-prompt evaluation is unreliable and commonly (mis)practiced: "most prior work also evaluated LLMs using only a single prompt ... Minor prompt variations can lead to diverse outcomes for different models" (Section 3.3.2) — a caution against over-trusting one BFCL/IFEval prompt template for the pre-registered comparison.
- Automated parsing-script grading can silently misscore: "evaluating LLMs entirely with an automated approach based on the answer extracted using parsing scripts may lead to an error of up to more than 10% difference in many tasks" (Section 3.2.3) — directly relevant risk for automatic schema-validity/flag-rate scoring if the parser itself has bugs.
- Undocumented decoding-parameter choices risk unintentional test-set overfitting: "This poses the risk of optimizing the parameters on test sets to improve performance" (Section 3.2.2).

## Metric / eval choices

- Recommends checking tokenizer vocabulary coverage of selected benchmarks as a robustness check (Table 4 recommendations row, "Robustness: Check tokenizer vocabulary coverage in selected benchmarks").
- Recommends documenting and justifying decoding parameters (temperature etc.) rather than leaving them unstated, and testing multiple settings on at least a subset (Section 4, "Response Generation").
- Recommends measuring runtime latency/GPU usage with tools like pyNVML alongside accuracy metrics, treating the accuracy-latency trade-off as part of "robust" evaluation (Section 4) — relevant analog for this project's own throughput-parity requirement on the 4-bit build.
- Warns that MMLU-style multiple-choice automated rankings can disagree with human-preference rankings (Chatbot Arena) and even with each other across implementations (Table 3) — a general reason not to treat any single guardrail metric (e.g. MMLU-Pro) as fully trustworthy on its own.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper's central concerns (prompt/decoding documentation, parsing-script validation, LLM-judge bias) are evaluation-integrity hygiene, not training methodology — it gives no guidance on LoRA vs. full SFT, DPO/GRPO reward design, or how to instill "flag rather than assert" behavior, so it doesn't help settle any of this project's core method questions.
- No content in the paper was directed at the reader as an instruction; it reads throughout as a normal academic survey/citation review, so nothing here needed to be disregarded on those grounds.
