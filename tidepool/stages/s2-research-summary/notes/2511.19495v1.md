# 2511.19495v1 — A Systematic Study of Compression Ordering for Large Language Models

**Relevance:** medium
Confirms a pipeline-ordering constraint relevant to the candidate ladder's final quantization step, but the study is on generic chat quality (not tool-calling/structured output), uses BitsAndBytes NF4 rather than GGUF, and is a small, somewhat rough academic study on Qwen2.5-3B.

## TL;DR

The paper compresses Qwen2.5-3B with knowledge distillation (KD), structured pruning (P), and 4-bit NF4 quantization (Q), testing all six orderings of the three techniques plus single-technique baselines. It finds quantization must come last: any ordering that quantizes early and then dequantizes to keep training collapses in quality, while Pruning → KD → Quantization (P-KD-Q) gives the best quality/compression trade-off (3.68x) among the multi-technique pipelines.

## Techniques to try

- Do quantization strictly last in a compression/training pipeline, after any training-based step (KD, fine-tuning): "BitsAndBytes 4-bit quantization produces inference-only models... This constraint is critical for determining valid compression orderings, as any technique requiring training (KD or post-pruning fine-tuning) must occur before final quantization." (Section 2.1.3) — directly supports the project's candidate-ladder ordering of putting "quantization-aware distillation, then quantization-aware training" after LoRA/full SFT/DPO/GRPO rather than interleaving it earlier.
- If pruning is ever used, prune before distillation/fine-tuning rather than after: the paper's best multi-technique order is "Pruning → Knowledge Distillation → Quantization (P-KD-Q)... achieving a $3.68\times$ compression ratio while preserving strong instruction-following and language understanding capabilities" (Abstract).

## Baselines & datasets used

- Base/teacher-student setup: Qwen2.5-3B as the compressed student, Qwen2.5-7B as KD teacher loaded in 8-bit via BitsAndBytes ("Knowledge distillation was performed using Qwen2.5-7B as the teacher model", Section 2.1.1) — a same-family, one-size-up teacher, which is a plausible pattern for other model families but not directly reusable here (no Qwen involved in our project).
- Training/calibration data: HuggingFaceH4/ultrachat_200k, "10% of the training split (approximately 10000 samples)" for KD and 2000 samples for pruning (500 for importance scoring, 1500 for post-pruning fine-tuning) (Section 3.1.2).
- Evaluation data: SQuAD (Stanford Question Answering Dataset) for generating evaluation prompts/contexts (Section 3.1.2) — a reading-comprehension dataset, not a tool-calling or instruction-following benchmark, so not comparable to BFCL/IFEval.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Early quantization followed by forced dequantization to continue training causes severe, largely irreversible damage: "Dequantization introduces information loss and potential weight corruption due to the irreversible nature of quantization... Sequences with early quantization (Q-*) test the hypothesis that this quality degradation significantly impacts subsequent compression effectiveness." (Section 2.3) The measured effect is dramatic — Q-first sequences (Q-P-KD, Q-KD-P, KD-Q-P) hit perplexities of 24–53 versus ~5 for the best direct sequence, and G-Eval scores collapse to 0.06–0.15 versus 0.73 for P-KD-Q (Table 1).
- Pruning alone is costly on its own: pruning at a 30% ratio degraded G-Eval from 0.830 (base) to 0.650 and prompt alignment from 0.670 to 0.330, with perplexity rising from 3.418 to 5.086 (Table 1) — a caution against relying on structured pruning as a lightweight technique in isolation.
- The paper itself flags diminishing returns from stacking techniques: "Moving from $3\times$ (Q alone) to $3.68\times$ (P-KD-Q) provides only $0.68\times$ additional compression while adding significant complexity, suggesting limited practical benefit of adding pruning to quantization-based workflows." (Section 3.3) — relevant caution if the project ever considers adding structured pruning to the quantization-only plan; the marginal compression gain may not be worth the added pipeline complexity and risk.

## Metric / eval choices

- Quality metrics used: G-Eval (LLM-as-judge, "Google's Gemini model is used as the judge model", correctness and relevance criteria), Clarity (composite of fluency via DistilGPT2 perplexity, contextual coherence via sentence-embedding cosine similarity, and Flesch Reading Ease readability), Prompt Alignment (LLM-as-judge adherence to prompt template), and raw perplexity (Section 3.2). None of these are structured-output, schema-validity, or tool-call-execution metrics, so they don't transfer to the project's BFCL/schema-validity/flag-rate targets — this paper cannot answer the reader brief's question about whether quantization damages structured output more than knowledge.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper does not test quantization at a scale, format (GGUF/llama.cpp), or bit-width matching our target (4-bit GGUF under 1.5GB), and only uses BitsAndBytes NF4; whether the "quantize last" ordering finding transfers to a GGUF-targeted quantization-aware distillation/training step is untested here.
- No structured-output, tool-calling, or instruction-following-under-quantization metric is reported, so this paper gives no direct evidence on the project's central question of whether low-bit quantization disproportionately harms structured output versus general knowledge.
- The paper has an internal inconsistency (Section 3.3 refers to "the base Qwen2.5-7B model" as the one being compressed, while the rest of the paper and Table 1 identify the base/compressed model as Qwen2.5-3B) — a sign the study may not have been rigorously proofread, which should temper confidence in the specific numbers.
