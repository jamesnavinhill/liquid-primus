# 2511.22138v1 — TinyLLM: Evaluation and Optimization of Small Language Models for Agentic Tasks on Edge Devices

**Relevance:** medium
Gives useful comparative context on BFCL performance and SFT/DPO/RL trade-offs for sub-3B agentic models, but its headline numbers are off-the-shelf model comparisons (not the authors' own trained/optimized model), it never touches quantization, and its own optimization pipeline (DPO on AgentBank) is explicitly unfinished.

## TL;DR

The paper benchmarks existing small models (TinyAgent-1.1B, TinyLlama-1.1B, Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B/4B, xLAM-2-1B/3B) on BFCL for tool-calling, finding a steep cliff below ~1.7B params, and separately describes (but has not finished) a DPO pipeline built from AgentBank data with TinyLlama outputs as the "rejected" side of preference pairs. It is a landscape/survey-style report rather than a validated method paper — useful for framing expectations, not for a technique we can lift and apply directly.

## Techniques to try

- Building DPO preference pairs cheaply from existing SFT data by pairing a stronger dataset's "chosen" response with a weaker model's generation as "rejected," rather than hand-labeling: "the AgentBank response serves as the chosen entity, and rejected responses are generated by TinyLlama/TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0. Manual validation confirmed the rejected outputs are consistently lower quality." (Section VIII) — conceptually adaptable to generating malformed-tool-return preference pairs, though our project needs schema-violation pairs specifically, not just "weaker model" outputs.
- Their qualitative ranking of methods matches our candidate ladder ordering and is worth citing as independent support: "supervised fine-tuning (SFT) offers limited gains and struggles with complex multi-function tasks. Preference-based methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) improve output stability and alignment, while reinforcement learning (e.g., PPO) is effective but computationally demanding—less ideal for edge deployment." (Section X)

## Baselines & datasets used

- Gorilla OpenFunctions dataset "expanded from 100 to 2,000 data points," spanning 40 sub-domains, used as their BFCL-style evaluation set (Section VI) — a candidate reference for dataset composition/category weighting if we want to sanity-check our own eval mix (their split: ~60% AST-based, 12% relevance detection, 10% chatting, ~7% execution, small SQL/Java/JS/REST slices).
- Models compared: TinyAgent-1.1B, TinyLlama-1.1B-32k-Instruct, Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B/4B (prompt-based function calling), xLAM-2-1b-fc-r and xLAM-2-3b-fc-r (native function-calling variants) — all evaluated with BFCL, no models in our exact 1.2B / hybrid-conv-attention class (LFM2.5) are included.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Ultra-compact models close to our target's scale fail badly on BFCL without dedicated optimization: TinyLlama-1.1B-32k-Instruct and TinyAgent-1.1B both score "only about 19.7% overall accuracy, with non-live accuracy plateauing at 20% and no success at all on multi-turn tasks" (Section IX-A) — a cautionary data point that base-model quality/architecture may matter more than scale alone, since our 1.2B base is claimed at 49.12 BFCLv3 (much higher than these 1.1B models here), and this paper's own SFT attempt on similar-size models also underperformed.
- "Initial supervised fine-tuning (SFT) results were not particularly impressive, motivating exploration of preference-based and RL-based methods." (Section IV-C) — a plain negative result for plain SFT at this scale, relevant to the first rung of our candidate ladder.
- Apparent gameability of the relevance/abstention metric: the two worst models score 0% on every functional category yet "these smaller models only succeed in irrelevance detection, where they abstain correctly" (Section IX-C), reaching 100% on Irrelevance Detection in Table III — consistent with our project's worry that a flag/abstain metric can be gamed by a model that essentially never commits to a call; worth remembering when interpreting our own flag-rate/false-flag-rate pair.
- The paper's own headline abstract numbers ("achieving up to 65.74% overall accuracy, and 55.62% multi-turn accuracy with hybrid optimization") are actually just the off-the-shelf xLAM-2-3b-fc-r baseline's scores from Table I, not a result of the authors' own fine-tuning/DPO/hybrid pipeline — their DPO training is explicitly incomplete ("We completed conversion of the ALFRED dataset from AgentBank and successfully performed DPO training; work on ALFWorld is in progress," Section VIII). Treat the abstract's framing as overstated relative to what was actually demonstrated.
- Internal inconsistency in the paper's own tables: Table I lists Qwen3-4B (Prompt) multi-turn accuracy as 35.25%, while Table IV lists the same model/metric as 16.88% (with Table IV's "Overall" column also duplicating xLAM-2-1b-fc-r's row values in place of Qwen3-4B's). This reduces confidence in the precision of any single number pulled from this paper.

## Metric / eval choices

- Uses BFCL over T-Eval specifically because BFCL needs no per-model meta_template and "enables plug-and-play evaluation by specifying a model name and receiving standardized outputs" (Section V-A) — supports our own choice to standardize on BFCL(v3) rather than a more bespoke framework.
- Reports three metric families per BFCL category: AST accuracy, executable evaluation, and unweighted overall accuracy across subcategories (Section V-C), plus a live/non-live/multi-turn split — the same category taxonomy (simple, multiple, parallel, parallel-multiple, relevance detection) our BFCLv3 target numbers are drawn from, useful for decomposing where our post-trained model gains or loses points.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper cites "bfcl2025v4" as its benchmark version while our target is BFCLv3 — category definitions and scoring may have shifted between versions, so any cross-reference of absolute numbers should be treated as directional, not a strict apples-to-apples comparison.
- None of the models here address 4-bit quantization, GGUF export, or throughput — it offers no evidence either way on this project's central open question of whether low-bit quantization damages structured output more than knowledge.
- No discussion of vocabulary size effects on tool-call/JSON tokenization, and no architecture resembling LFM2.5's hybrid conv+GQA design is evaluated, so model-selection guidance here (Qwen, TinyLlama, Falcon, Gemma, Phi candidates) doesn't transfer directly to our fixed base model.
