# 2601.15657v1 — Integrating Knowledge Distillation Methods: A Sequential Multi-Stage Framework

**Relevance:** low
Real topical overlap with "knowledge distillation" and "prevent forgetting while training up a narrow skill," but every experiment is CNN image classification (CIFAR-100, Tiny ImageNet), not autoregressive LLM tool-calling/instruction-following, and there is no quantization angle at all.

## TL;DR

The paper proposes SMSKD, a way to combine several classic knowledge-distillation losses (response-based, feature-based, relation-based) by applying them one at a time in sequential training stages rather than summing them, and uses a frozen copy of the student from the previous stage as a KL-divergence anchor to stop the student forgetting earlier-stage knowledge when the distillation objective changes. It's evaluated only on small CNNs (ResNet/WRN/VGG) doing 100–200-way image classification, so it's a compression/integration technique paper, not an LLM post-training or quantization paper.

## Techniques to try

- Sequential multi-stage distillation instead of naively summing distillation losses, to avoid "optimization conflicts" between objectives with different scales/gradients: "Directly combining them without proper adaptation can lead to optimization conflicts and suboptimal performance." (Section 2.3) — conceptually relevant if we ever stage SFT → DPO → GRPO with different loss types and worry about one stage undoing another.
- Anchor each new training stage to a frozen copy of the model from the end of the previous stage via a KL penalty, explicitly modeled on PPO's fixed baseline: "the reference model, inspired by the use of fixed baselines in reinforcement learning (e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization [19]), is introduced in our SMSKD framework and acts as a stabilizing anchor that constrains the optimization trajectory of the student model." (Section 3.2) — this is essentially the same idea as the KL-to-reference-policy term already standard in DPO/PPO-style RLHF, so it is not a new tool for our ladder, just a confirming data point that the pattern generalizes.
- Weight the anchor/reference loss per-sample by the reference model's own confidence in the ground-truth class (true class probability, TCP), rather than a flat weight, so the anchor is trusted less on samples the reference model is unsure about: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{AdaRef}}=\text{TCP}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{Ref}}$ (Eq. 9, Section 3.2).

## Baselines & datasets used

- CIFAR-100 (100-class, 32×32) and Tiny ImageNet (200-class, 64×64) image classification; teacher-student pairs ResNet56→ResNet20, WRN40_2→WRN16_2, VGG13→VGG8 (Section 4.1).
- Compared against SAKD (spot-adaptive KD, layer-routing) and "Direct Loss Aggregation" (naive sum of distillation losses) as multi-source baselines (Section 4.1.3).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Naive loss summation (DLA) and even the more sophisticated SAKD baseline show negative transfer in some settings, e.g. "CRD degrading from 75.49 to 75.33/75.18" when integrated with KD under WRN40_2→WRN16_2 (Section 4.2.1) — a caution that just adding two training objectives/losses together can actively hurt a metric that was fine standalone.
- Combining two distillation methods isn't always additive: "A rare exception occurs in the combination of CRD and VID, where performance falls slightly below that of CRD alone." (Section 4.2.2), attributed to the two methods pulling features in opposite directions.
- The anchor/reference loss has a sweet spot, not "more is better": "Further increasing λr from 0.5 to 0.8 yields minimal gains and can even degrade performance, suggesting that an excessively strong reference constraint may overly bias the model toward previously learned knowledge while limiting its capacity to acquire new knowledge from the second stage." (Section 4.3.2) — relevant if we ever tune a KL-to-base-model penalty to fight capability erosion; too strong a penalty can suppress the new skill instead of just protecting the old one.
- TCP-based adaptive weighting isn't universally beneficial: it improved response/feature-based combinations but "slight drops appear in RKD (75.28 → 74.99) and PKT (75.66 → 75.09)," attributed to relation-based objectives being less aligned with a class-confidence signal (Section 4.3.3).
- Diminishing/negative returns from adding more stages: beyond two or three stages, "the student model approaches its capacity limit ... and additional stages provide limited benefit while increasing training cost" (Section 4.5) — a caution against assuming more sequential fine-tuning stages always help.

## Metric / eval choices

- Primary metric is plain top-1 test accuracy (%) on the classification benchmarks (Tables 1–3).
- To quantify forgetting/retention directly (rather than just net accuracy), they track the overlap between the sets of correctly-classified samples before/after a stage transition using set intersection-over-union: "$\text{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}\times 100\%$" (Section 4.3.2) — a reusable pattern for measuring "did the new training stage break things the old model got right" that could translate to tracking per-example regressions on our guardrail evals (IFEval/MMLU-Pro/etc.) between training stages, rather than only comparing aggregate scores.

## Open questions for our project

- Does the reference-model KL-anchor + confidence-weighting pattern add anything beyond what a standard KL-to-base-model penalty in DPO/GRPO already gives us for keeping general ability from eroding, or is it redundant with what we're already planning?
- No results here are at LLM scale, in text/sequence generation, or under any quantization — nothing in this paper speaks to whether low-bit quantization damages structured output/instruction-following more than knowledge, or to tool-call schema validity, so it does not help ground any of the project's specific numeric targets.
- No sign of embedded instructions or unusual directives in the paper text; it reads as a standard academic KD paper.
