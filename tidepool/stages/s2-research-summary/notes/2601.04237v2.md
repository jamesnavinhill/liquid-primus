# 2601.04237v2 — SAGE-32B: Agentic Reasoning via Iterative Distillation

**Relevance:** medium
Nothing here settles a decision at our scale, but the hard-negative taxonomy for malformed tool calls and the distillation-then-DPO-then-RL training ladder map closely onto our own candidate ladder, and the reported "alignment tax" is a useful cautionary data point for our guardrail-drift budget.

## TL;DR

SAGE-32B is a 32B model (built on Qwen2.5-32B) fine-tuned for agentic tool-use via a teacher-distillation-then-DPO-then-RL pipeline, plus a novel "Inverse Reasoning" critique head. It reports large tool-calling reliability gains (argument hallucination rate 14.5% → 5.2% → 2.4% across training stages) but also a measurable general-knowledge/guardrail regression from tool-use fine-tuning, at a scale and with data/weights availability that don't transfer to our 1.2B on-device setting.

## Techniques to try

- Generate labeled "hard negative" tool calls for training a flag/repair signal, using a fixed taxonomy: ("For every correct API call, we generate 3 "hard negatives": Type Error: String instead of Integer. Hallucinated Key: Extra parameter not in schema. Logic Error: Correct syntax, valid parameter, but nonsensical value" — §3.1). This taxonomy (type error / hallucinated key / logic error) is a concrete, reusable template for constructing our own malformed-tool-return probe set.
- DPO stage specifically to penalize compliant execution of unsafe tool calls: ("The final stage aligns the model to refuse unsafe tool requests (e.g., delete_database()). We use DPO with a β=0.1 coefficient to penalize compliance with malicious prompts." — §3.1.2).
- RL reward shaped directly against argument hallucination, layered after SFT/distillation rather than replacing it: ("Argument Hallucination Penalty: Heavy penalties for inventing parameters not present in the tool definition." — §3.1.3), with the ablation showing distillation alone cuts hallucination from 14.5%→5.2% and RL adds a further cut to 2.4% (§6.5) — i.e. distillation teaches the strategy, RL enforces the syntax.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Standard benchmarks: MMLU, MMLU-Pro, GSM8K, MATH(-500), MMMLU, MGSM, AgentBench, GPQA (all at 32B/70B scale, Qwen2.5-32B / Llama-3-70B / GPT-4-Turbo comparators — not below-8B).
- Internal "Enterprise-500" suite: 500 real-world workflows spanning "SQL generation, JSON parsing, API chaining" (§6.5) — topically close to our own SQL-dialect / JSON-Schema specialization target, but it's a private, undocumented eval set, not something we can reuse directly.
- Training data: a synthetic 5M-trajectory agentic dataset distilled from DeepSeek as teacher over OSWorld and WebArena environments — proprietary and far larger than anything feasible for a 1.2B on-device project; not available to us.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Fine-tuning for strict tool-use syntax measurably regressed general/knowledge benchmarks even at 32B: MATH dropped "-3.46% compared to the Qwen base" and GPQA regressed "-2.5% vs Qwen Base" in the standard (non-voting) model, which the authors call the "Alignment Tax" (§6.3) — a direct precedent for the risk our guardrail-metric budget (±2.0 points) is meant to catch.
- A documented failure mode ("Ambiguity Loop"): the agent got stuck retrying a malformed tool call with only superficial variations ("Retry sentiment_tool(input=JSON[0])... Retry sentiment_tool(input=JSON[1])") instead of reading the error message holistically, until it exhausted its context window (§7.2) — a concrete illustration that syntax-recovery training does not by itself instill "flag rather than assert" behavior when a tool signature is ambiguous.
- Reported domain overfitting: "the model performs poorly on medical or legal reasoning tasks, often hallucinating non-existent tools or regulations in those domains" (§8) because training data was concentrated on coding/data-analysis/file manipulation — relevant caution for a project that also intends to specialize narrowly to a particular tech stack.
- Claimed 128k context support is undercut by its own limitations section: "processing full history for every token generation becomes prohibitively slow past 32k tokens on standard hardware. Agent loops exceeding 50 turns often hit timeouts" (§8).

## Metric / eval choices

- "Internal Recovery Rate (IRR): The percentage of episodes where the agent successfully corrects a deliberately injected tool error (e.g., parameter mismatch or timeout) within 2 turns" (§6.1) — conceptually close to our own held-out malformed-tool-return probe, though here it measures recovery-after-injection rather than a flag/false-flag pair.
- The paper leans heavily on majority-vote-at-k=32 "Thinking" mode scores as its headline numbers, and is explicit that its one-shot (Pass@1) numbers are lower than the voting numbers (e.g. MATH 78.9% one-shot vs 91.8% with k=32 voting, §6.3) — a reminder to keep our own first-attempt (no retry, no voting) metric cleanly separated from any voting/self-consistency numbers we might be tempted to quote.

## Open questions for our project

- This paper's rigor is questionable: it reports formal "theorems" whose proofs are essentially restatements of assumptions, an unreleased model ("we are not releasing the full model weights publicly at this time"), an unnamed/unverifiable proprietary teacher and dataset, and self-citations to the same small group's prior work. We should not treat its specific percentages (e.g. 2.4% argument-hallucination rate, 76% vs 35% IRR) as reliable priors — only the qualitative structure (negative-sampling taxonomy, staged SFT→DPO→RL, alignment-tax framing) seems worth carrying forward.
- Nothing in the paper's text reads as an instruction addressed to the reader/agent; no prompt-injection concerns found.
