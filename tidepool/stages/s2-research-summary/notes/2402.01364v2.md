# 2402.01364v2 — Continual Learning for Large Language Models: A Survey

**Relevance:** medium
It names concrete mitigation techniques and delta-style evaluation metrics for exactly the "how do we keep general ability from eroding while training a narrow tool-calling skill" question the brief raises, but it is a pure survey with no original experiments, no numbers at any usable scale, and nothing validated on tool-calling or quantization specifically.

## TL;DR

This is a taxonomy paper, not an experimental one: it organizes prior work on updating LLMs over time into continual pre-training, continual instruction tuning, and continual alignment, and catalogs methods and benchmarks for each. For this project it's most useful as a pointer list — named techniques (replay, orthogonal LoRA subspaces, mixed data ratios) and named failure modes (safety/instruction-following erosion from fine-tuning) that map onto the "don't let guardrail metrics move more than 2 points" design goal, even though none of it is demonstrated at 1.2B or on tool/schema tasks.

## Techniques to try

- Mixed-ratio data composition across the old and new training distributions, as a lever against forgetting when specializing: "Dong et al. (2023) analyze the LLMs continuously tuned on different domains and find that the sequence of training data has a significant impact on the performance of LLMs. They also offer a Mixed Fine-tuning (DMT) strategy to learn multiple abilities in different domains." — Section 4.2
- Orthogonal-subspace LoRA to limit interference between the new (tool-calling) skill and the base model's existing abilities: "O-LoRA incrementally learns new tasks in an orthogonal subspace while fixing the LoRA parameters learned from past tasks to minimize catastrophic forgetting." — Section 4.1
- Replay/rehearsal with a stored buffer of prior-stage data during fine-tuning, cited repeatedly as a forgetting mitigant (e.g. "Contunual-T0 Scialom et al. (2022) employs rehearsal with a memory buffer... to store previous tasks data and replay them during training." — Section 4.1). Worth considering as a cheap first defense for the LoRA/full-SFT rungs of the candidate ladder.

## Baselines & datasets used

- None run by this paper itself. It surveys TRACE (Wang et al. 2023b, "encompasses eight diverse datasets covering specialized domains, multilingual tasks, code generation, and mathematical reasoning" — Section 6.1), CITB (SuperNI-based, 1,600+ NLP tasks — Section 6.2), and COPF's use of SHP/HH preference datasets (Section 6.3) — none of these are tool-calling or structured-output benchmarks, so not directly reusable, but TRACE's evaluation protocol (below) is.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Fine-tuning degrades safety alignment even without adversarial intent, a direct risk analog for any SFT/DPO/GRPO stage on top of an aligned base: "previous studies Qi et al. (2023) also demonstrate that the behavior of safely aligned LLMs can be easily affected and degraded by instruction tuning." — Section 7.2
- Instruction tuning on a narrow stream can erode general knowledge, the core failure mode this project's guardrail metrics (MMLU-Pro, GPQA Diamond) are designed to catch: "Large language models continually trained on different stages can experience the issue of unconscious forgetting Lin et al. (2023), which shows that continual instruction tuning can erode the LLM's general knowledge." — Section 7.2
- The survey itself flags that continual-alignment benchmarking is essentially absent: "there is a notable absence of dedicated benchmarks for continual alignment, presenting an opportunity for future research" — Section 6.3, i.e. don't expect an off-the-shelf benchmark for measuring alignment/preference drift from this literature.

## Metric / eval choices

- TRACE's delta-style metrics are a close conceptual match to this project's "stay within 2 points of a matched base rerun" design: General Ability Delta, Instruction Following Delta, and Safety Delta are each defined as "the performance difference of an LLM on general tasks after training on sequential target tasks" (GAD, Eq. 4), the corresponding instruction-following change (IFD, Eq. 5), and "the safety variation of a model's response after sequential training" (SD, Eq. 6) — Section 7.2. Framing our IFEval/IFBench/Multi-IF/MMLU-Pro/GPQA guardrail deltas explicitly as before/after deltas against a matched base, rather than absolute scores, mirrors this.
- Also names Forward Transfer and Backward Transfer as standard continual-learning metrics (Eqs. 1–2, Section 7.1) — less directly applicable since this project trains one narrow skill rather than a task sequence, but BWT's framing (performance on "old tasks" before vs. after learning the new one) is the same shape as our guardrail-metric check.

## Open questions for our project

- None of the cited mitigation techniques (DMT, O-LoRA, replay) have been shown on tool-calling, JSON/schema generation, or at 1.2B scale — it's unclear whether they'd move the needle on BFCL or first-attempt schema validity versus just doing a good data-mix ablation directly, which is already on our candidate ladder.
- The survey provides no guidance on quantization interacting with any of this, so it doesn't inform the 4-bit GGUF / throughput questions at all.
- No instruction-like text directed at a reader was found in the paper; it is a standard academic survey and reads as description throughout.
