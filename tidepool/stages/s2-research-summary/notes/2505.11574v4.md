# 2505.11574v4 — Quantization Meets Reasoning: Exploring and Mitigating Degradation of Low-Bit LLMs in Mathematical Reasoning

**Relevance:** medium
Directly relevant grounding on how low-bit PTQ hurts small models' procedural/execution skill and a cheap post-quant DPO recovery recipe, but the quantization methods studied (AWQ/GPTQ/SmoothQuant weight-only or W8A8) and target task (math reasoning) differ from this project's GGUF k-quant + tool-calling/structured-output target, so it informs rather than settles the plan.

## TL;DR

The paper studies PTQ (AWQ, GPTQ, SmoothQuant) on Qwen2.5/LLaMA-3 models from 0.5–7B on math benchmarks, finding that quantization disproportionately increases "method" and "execution" (procedural/arithmetic) errors rather than conceptual ones, that failures emerge in the earliest reasoning step and cascade, and that smaller models degrade far more than larger ones. They then show a cheap fix: locate the first error step per failing example, build DPO preference pairs from truncated failures completed by a stronger model ("Silver Bullet" data), and fine-tune the quantized model with LoRA/QLoRA DPO — recovering most of the lost accuracy with as few as 332 examples and 3–5 minutes on one GPU.

## Techniques to try

- Post-quantization DPO recovery with a tiny, targeted, error-localized dataset rather than generic supervision: "as few as 332 curated examples and 3-5 minutes of compute on a single GPU recover 4-bit weight math reasoning toward the full-precision baseline while preserving PTQ efficiency" (Abstract). Directly relevant to the project's quantization-aware distillation/QAT step in the candidate ladder — worth trying before committing to full QAT.
- Build recovery-training pairs by truncating a failing quantized-model trace exactly at its first wrong step and having a stronger model complete it correctly, rather than using full from-scratch solutions: "we construct a targeted counterexample dataset by truncating the incorrect reasoning traces precisely at the identified erroneous steps... prompt powerful baseline models... to resume and complete these truncated solutions" (§3.2.2). This "resume-from-first-error" construction beat both random sampling and full-solution positives by a wide margin (§4.4/4.5) — an analogous strategy could apply to constructing DPO pairs for malformed tool-call recovery: locate exactly where a response would fail (schema violation point) rather than contrasting whole responses.
- DPO recipe used: LoRA/QLoRA rank 32 adapters, cosine LR schedule, base LR 1e-6, warmup ratio 0.1, global batch size 8, β=1 sigmoid preference loss — "we set the LoRA rank to 32 for every injected adapter matrix... base learning rate 1×10⁻⁶, warm-up ratio 0.1... under a global batch size of 8" (§3.2.3). A concrete, cheap starting hyperparameter set for a small-scale post-quant DPO pass, though it was tuned for 0.5–7B math models, not a 1.2B tool-calling model.
- Automated error-labeling pipeline (5-model judge ensemble + majority vote + human arbitration only on disagreements) reached 97.2% agreement with human ground truth on ~9,908 cases (§3.1.3) — a template for cheaply building a large, reliable labeled dataset (e.g., for the malformed-tool-return flag/false-flag probe) without exhaustive human annotation.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Quantization methods: AWQ and GPTQ (4-bit weight, 16-bit activation, W4A16) and SmoothQuant (8-bit weight+activation, W8A8), applied to Qwen2.5 and LLaMA-3/3.2 Instruct models at 0.5B, 1B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B, 8B (Table 1), plus Qwen3-8B/14B/32B (Appendix E.1).
- Math benchmarks: GSM8K (8,500 grade-school problems), MATH (12,500 competition problems, evaluated via disjoint MATH-500 for leakage-free recovery evaluation), AIME (90 problems, 2022–2025).
- General-capability benchmarks for contrast: MMLU, ARC-Easy/Challenge, HellaSwag, CommonSenseQA, IFEval (Appendix E.2).
- Format-alignment step uses PRM800K (800K step-level correctness annotations, 75K solutions, 12K problems) with LoRA/QLoRA, purely to standardize step-by-step output format before measuring degradation, not to teach new math knowledge.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Smaller models suffer much more from quantization: "Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct model experiences accuracy drops exceeding 60% post-quantization, whereas the larger Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model incurs only a modest degradation of approximately 2–3%" (§4.1). Concretely, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Inst dropped from 23.98 to 7.24 (GPTQ, -69.81%) on MATH (Table 1). This is a strong warning sign for a 1.2B model being pushed to 4-bit — expect proportionally larger degradation than papers reporting only 7B+ results.
- Naive (non-targeted) recovery fine-tuning underperforms badly: the "ALL-NonStep" variant (full gold solutions, no resuming from the first error) scored 51.83 average vs. 57.42 for the step-aligned "ALL-S" variant across GSM8K/MATH500/MMLU (Table 2) — "training on our error-targeted stepwise All split consistently outperforms both size-matched random supervision... and the non-stepwise variant... On average, All improves accuracy by about 1.7 points over Random and by about 5.6 points over Non-Step" (§4.5). Generic supervision without localizing the failure point is a documented dead end for this kind of small-data recovery training.
- Degradation is highly task/complexity dependent: "quantized models exhibited relatively minor accuracy losses on the simpler GSM8K benchmark, with an average performance decline of only 7.16%. In contrast, the MATH dataset incurred a more pronounced average degradation of 15.18%" (§4.1), and general-capability benchmarks degrade far less than math: IFEval average relative drop was only -5.01% and -2.61 points, versus MATH's -29.84% and -10.56 points (Table 4, Appendix E.2). This suggests instruction-following-style tasks may be more quantization-robust than complex multi-step procedural tasks — but the project's tool-calling target is itself procedural/execution-heavy (schema construction, exact syntax), so this analogy should not be over-trusted without direct measurement.
- Restoration training was only validated on W4A16/W8A8 PTQ via AWQ/GPTQ/SmoothQuant on 0.5–8B dense transformer models; it was not tested on GGUF-style k-quant formats or on hybrid conv/attention architectures like LFM2.5, so transfer to this project's actual deployment format is unconfirmed.

## Metric / eval choices

- Uses a hierarchical 4-category, 7-subtype error taxonomy (Conceptual, Method, Execution, Reasoning errors) attributed to the *first* erroneous step in a step-structured chain-of-thought trace, rather than a single pass/fail score — directly analogous in spirit to this project's need to classify tool-call failures (e.g., schema-invalid vs. semantically-wrong vs. correctly-flagged).
- Reports both absolute accuracy point drop and relative percentage drop per benchmark, and stratifies by task difficulty (MATH Levels 1–5) and subject domain (Table 7/8) — degradation is not uniform, and worsens monotonically with difficulty, which argues for evaluating quantization impact on this project's harder/longer tool-schemas separately from simple ones rather than only an aggregate BFCL score.

## Open questions for our project

- Does the "method/execution errors dominate over conceptual errors" finding transfer from math CoT to structured tool-call generation, where "execution" more directly maps to schema/syntax correctness? This paper doesn't test tool calling or JSON/structured output directly.
- Would the same measure→locate→restore DPO recipe (locate first schema-violating token, build a preference pair against a corrected completion) work for recovering a 4-bit GGUF LFM2.5 tool-calling model, given the recipe was only validated on W4A16/W8A8 PTQ of dense 0.5–8B math models?
- The paper's IFEval result (small relative degradation under quantization) is a data point suggesting instruction-following is relatively quantization-robust compared to multi-step math reasoning; it's worth checking whether this generalizes to BFCL/schema-validity metrics or whether tool-calling behaves more like the fragile "execution" category.
- No instruction-like text directed at the reader was found in the paper; Appendix G ("The Usage of LLM") is a disclosure of the authors' own LLM usage for annotation/editing, not an instruction to any downstream reader — noted here only because the brief asked to flag such content, not because any was found.
