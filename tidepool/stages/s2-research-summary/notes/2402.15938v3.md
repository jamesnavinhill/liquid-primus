# 2402.15938v3 — Generalization or Memorization: Data Contamination and Trustworthy Evaluation for Large Language Models

**Relevance:** medium
Not a training-method or quantization paper, but it offers a cheap, black-box-compatible sanity check (CDD/TED) that could be used to verify the pre-registered BFCLv3/IFEval deltas aren't inflated by benchmark leakage into the SFT/DPO/GRPO data mix.

## TL;DR

The paper proposes CDD, a method that detects whether an LLM has memorized a specific test example purely from sampled outputs (no access to logits or training data): it samples repeatedly at temperature 0.8, measures edit-distance similarity to the greedy (temperature 0) output, and flags contamination when that distribution is abnormally "peaked." It pairs this with TED, a post-hoc correction that strips near-duplicate/high-similarity samples from the evaluation pool to recover a less-inflated performance estimate. Both are validated on 7B-scale code-gen and reasoning models with simulated LoRA contamination, and applied to ChatGPT on HumanEval as a real-world case.

## Techniques to try

- CDD as a lightweight, black-box contamination probe for our own eval sets: for each test prompt, take one greedy sample plus n sampled outputs at temperature 0.8 and check the edit-distance peakedness; "CDD uses the sampled texts to identify the peakedness of LLM's output distribution for data contamination detection" (§1) — could be run against BFCLv3/IFEval items after SFT/DPO to catch inadvertent leakage from data-mix construction, without needing training-data access.
- TED-style correction before reporting a benchmark score: exclude near-duplicate high-similarity samples and dedupe repeats, then recompute the metric — "TED substantially mitigates performance improvements up to 66.9% attributed to data contamination across various contamination setups" (Abstract) — a candidate cross-check to see whether our BFCLv3 gain survives contamination-correction, not just raw Pass@1/accuracy.
- The method only needs sampled text, not probabilities or model weights, so it works even for closed/quantized deployments: "CDD... necessitates only the sampled texts to detect data contamination" (Abstract).

## Baselines & datasets used

- Detection baselines compared: N-gram (13-gram, char- and token-level), embedding similarity, perplexity, Min-k% Prob, and LLM Decontaminator (using ChatGPT as judge) (Table 2/3).
- Simulated-contamination base models are all ≤7B: CodeLlama-7B, CodeGen-6.7B, Llama2-7B, Bloom-7B, fine-tuned with LoRA on HumanEval (code) or GSM8K (logical reasoning) mixed into StarCoder/RedPajama data at ratios 1:{0, 0.1K, 1K, 10K} (Table 1, Appendix A).
- Two new benchmarks introduced: DetCon (2224 contamination-detection tasks) and ComiEval (560 contamination-mitigation tasks); real-world case study uses ChatGPT on HumanEval, a reconstructed HumanEval_R (translated/renamed), and a post-cutoff CodeForces2305 set.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- The simulated-contamination datasets were built with LoRA, not full fine-tuning, because of compute cost — the authors flag this as a limitation: "we employ a popular parameter-efficient fine-tuning approach, i.e., LoRA, instead of full-parameter fine-tuning to simulate data contamination for LLMs. In future work, we plan to attempt full-parameter fine-tuning" (§7 Limitations) — so results may not transfer cleanly if our own contamination risk arises from full/higher-rank SFT rather than LoRA.
- Detection and mitigation require many samples per test item (51 samples at temperature 0.8 in their setup) to estimate the output distribution reliably, which is compute overhead layered on top of normal eval: "our approaches require multiple samplings to compute the output distribution, and the more samplings conducted, the better the effect" (§7 Limitations).
- TED's "exclude peakedness" rule is the dominant contributor to mitigation but also "marginally decreases the performance of the uncontaminated model" as its threshold τ is raised (§4.3) — i.e., the correction is not free even when there is no real contamination, so it would need re-tuning (α, ξ, τ are all hand-set hyperparameters here) before trusting it on our own eval pipeline.
- Validation is limited to code generation and logical reasoning benchmarks only; the authors explicitly note this scope limitation (§7).

## Metric / eval choices

- Detection quality reported as Accuracy, F1 Score, and AUC (Table 3); CDD shows average relative improvements of 21.8%–30.2% over the next-best baseline on these three metrics combined, at 7B model scale.
- Mitigation evaluation uses Pass@1 (Chen et al. 2021) as the underlying task metric $\mathcal{E}$, with $\text{Pass@1}_{\text{TED}}$ compared against raw Pass@1 across contamination "occurrences" (simulated training epochs on leaked data).
- Introduces "Avg. Peak" (mean peakedness score) and "CR" (contaminated-ratio, fraction of tasks CDD flags as leaked) as reporting metrics for real-world black-box models like ChatGPT (Table 5).

## Open questions for our project

- Would CDD's peakedness signal be usable directly on BFCLv3/IFEval-style structured-output tasks, where the "correct" answer is a JSON/tool-call string rather than free-form text — does edit distance over tokenized tool-call strings behave the same way, or does the rigid schema itself create false peakedness even without contamination?
- Given our SFT/DPO/GRPO data mix specializes to the operator's own tool schemas, is there a realistic risk of near-duplicate BFCL-style examples leaking in, and would a CDD-style check on held-out BFCLv3 items be worth running once before reporting the final +3.0-point claim?
- No embedded instructions or prompt-injection attempts were found in this paper; it reads as a straightforward research paper throughout.
