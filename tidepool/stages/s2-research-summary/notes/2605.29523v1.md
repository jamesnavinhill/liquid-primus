# 2605.29523v1 — K-FinHallu: A Hallucination Detection Benchmark for Multi-Turn RAG in Korean Finance

**Relevance:** medium
The task, domain (Korean financial RAG hallucination *detection*), and model scale (8B judge/detector, not the 1.2B on-device model) don't match this project directly, but the rationale-supervised SFT recipe and the abstention-is-hardest finding speak directly to the "flag rather than assert" training-signal question.

## TL;DR

This paper builds a benchmark where an LLM must judge whether a RAG response is faithful, hallucinated, or a (correct/incorrect) refusal, across multi-turn Korean financial dialogues. The main transferable result: LoRA-fine-tuning Qwen3-8B to emit a templated chain-of-thought rationale before its class label beats fine-tuning on the label alone, and beats frontier closed models overall — but "justified abstention" (knowing when to refuse) stays the weakest skill for every model, tuned or not.

## Techniques to try

- Templated rationale supervision placed inside the model's native reasoning block, forcing a two-step judgment (assess answerability, then assess correctness/faithfulness) before the label: "The rationale is placed inside Qwen3's native <think> block so that the model is supervised to produce it during its thinking phase before emitting the class label." (Appendix E, Rationale template) — relevant analogue for teaching the model to check "is this tool result usable" before deciding whether to flag it.
- Ablation isolating the value of rationale supervision: "Qwen3-8B SFT without rationale scores 0.822" vs. "Qwen3-8B SFT-R" (with rationale) at 0.896 overall accuracy (Section 5.3, "Closing the Gap with Fine-tuning") — same LoRA setup, rationale-only difference, a clean data point for whether reasoning-supervision helps a small tuned model detect a bad/garbage input.
- LoRA config for an 8B model on a 4-class classification/detection task: "LoRA Hu et al. (2022) at rank $r{=}8$, $\alpha{=}32$, and a dropout of 0.05, attached to all linear projections of the backbone. We train for 3 epochs with a learning rate of $1\mathrm{e}{-4}$... The effective batch size is 32" (Appendix E, Optimization) — a concrete, cheap LoRA recipe (~4 GPU-hours) that could seed a first LoRA SFT pass for the flag/refuse behavior.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Source corpus: "Korean Financial and Legal Document Machine Reading Comprehension" from AI-Hub, split by source institution to avoid leakage (Section 4.1) — domain-specific, not reusable for a tool-calling/agent-gateway project.
- Detector baselines span Korean-centric (kanana-2-30b, EXAONE-4.0-32B), open-source (Llama-3.1 8B/70B, Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen3 8B/14B/32B) and closed-source (GPT-4o, GPT-5, Gemini-2.5 Flash/Pro) models, all evaluated zero-shot at temperature 0 (Section 5.2).
- Fine-tuning framework: ms-swift, LoRA, served with vLLM under tensor parallelism for inference (Appendix E).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Abstention/refusal is the hardest axis for every model, including the best fine-tuned one: "Refusal remains the weakest axis across all models," with the fine-tuned Qwen3-8B SFT-R topping out at 0.750 accuracy on that axis (Section 5.3) — a direct data point that "knowing when to say no" is harder to instill than ordinary correctness, relevant to the flag-rate/false-flag-rate sub-criterion.
- Closed-source frontier models systematically mishandle correct refusals: "Closed-source models detect False Refusal accurately but struggle to recognize True Refusal as a valid abstention, treating refusal itself as a negative signal" (Section 5.3) — a concrete failure mode (over-penalizing legitimate refusal) worth designing the flag/false-flag eval to catch.
- Fine-grained diagnosis is much harder than binary detection even after scaling: "a substantial drop from binary detection, confirming that diagnosing the cause of a hallucination is harder than detecting its presence," and one specific error type (Financial Term Misunderstanding) "shows minimal improvement even at 32B" (Section 5.4) — a caution that scaling alone doesn't fix certain narrow, jargon-dependent failure classes, plausibly analogous to schema/domain-specific tool-return errors.
- This is a domain-specific 8B-scale detector result (not the 1.2B on-device generation setting this project targets), and the task is "judge someone else's response as faithful/hallucinated," not "the acting model itself decides whether to flag a malformed tool return" — the transfer is by analogy only, not a directly reusable result.

## Metric / eval choices

- Binary hallucination detection reported as accuracy/precision/recall/F1, plus a four-class scheme (Faithful, Hallucination, False Refusal, True Refusal) collapsed to three classes for readability (Section 5.1) — a template for scoring "flag vs. false-flag" as a joint, non-gameable pair rather than a single number, matching this project's own flag-rate/false-flag-rate design.
- Gwet's AC1 used instead of Cohen's kappa for human/LLM-judge agreement, explicitly "to avoid the kappa paradox under high agreement with skewed class distributions" (Section 4.2, footnote) — worth considering for scoring the ~300-item malformed-tool-return probe, which will likely have a skewed well-formed/malformed split.

## Open questions for our project

- Does templated "assess-then-decide" rationale supervision (forcing the model to reason about input validity before answering) transfer from an 8B judge model down to a 1.2B on-device model with a much smaller vocabulary and hidden size, or does capacity limit how much reasoning-before-label helps at that scale?
- The paper's own finding that abstention is uniquely hard to teach (capping out at 0.750 even after supervised fine-tuning) raises the question of whether this project's 0.80 flag-rate / 0.10 false-flag-rate targets are optimistic for a much smaller model with less capacity for this kind of judgment.
