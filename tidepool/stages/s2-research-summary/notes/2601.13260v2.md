# 2601.13260v2 — Stop Taking Tokenizers for Granted: They Are Core Design Decisions in Large Language Models

**Relevance:** low
A conceptual position/survey paper on tokenizer design for multilingual fairness and reuse, with no empirical results, no code/JSON/tool-call analysis, and no quantization discussion, so it gives only weak background for this project's vocabulary and post-training questions.

## TL;DR

This is a position paper arguing that tokenizer design (algorithm choice, vocabulary size, reuse vs. retraining, and evaluation) should be treated as a first-class modeling decision rather than a fixed preprocessing step, mostly illustrated with multilingual/morphological fairness examples. It proposes a structured workflow and evaluation dimensions but explicitly provides no new empirical results or ablations, so it offers framing rather than numbers usable for this project's tool-calling/quantization targets.

## Techniques to try

- None directly applicable. The paper's "structured iterative process" (initial assessment → reuse decision → corpus curation → training → evaluation → iteration → documentation) is a general checklist for building a tokenizer from scratch or adapting one, not a technique for post-training a model on a fixed 65,536-token vocabulary ("Table 2: Core Stages in Tokenizer Development Process").

## Baselines & datasets used

- None — this is a survey/position paper with no experiments of its own; all numbers cited are pulled from other papers (e.g., Tao et al. 2024 on vocabulary scaling laws, Reddy et al. 2025 on tokenizer training-data diminishing returns).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Undertrained/anomalous vocabulary tokens can cause silent failures and erratic generation: "The notorious SolidGoldMagikarp token, which emerged in GPT models due to skewed data distributions, illustrates how poorly trained tokens can trigger erratic behavior" (§3.2.3) — a cautionary point if the 65,536-token LFM2.5 vocabulary contains rare/undertrained tokens that could destabilize structured output.
- Vocabulary expansion is a common but weakly justified fix: "Vocabulary expansion is a common but ad hoc remedy, with limited understanding of when it helps ... often yielding short-term gains that fail to generalize" (§3.2.2) — relevant only if this project ever considered growing the vocabulary rather than post-training on the existing one.
- Common intrinsic tokenizer metrics (fertility, compression, vocabulary size) correlate weakly with downstream task performance: "later findings show no consistent correlation between CTC, vocabulary size, and task accuracy" (§3.2.4).

## Metric / eval choices

- Proposes five "core evaluation dimensions" for tokenizers: coverage, generalizability, linguistic alignment, robustness (noise/code-switching), and representation utilization (Appendix C) — general-purpose diagnostic categories, not specific to JSON schema or tool-call token efficiency.
- Recommends reporting "tokens per 1k characters by language, parity ratios, compression–structure balance, and estimated pretrain FLOPs at fixed data budgets" (§3.2.4) rather than raw fertility/vocab-size numbers — could inform a lightweight tokens-per-character audit of LFM2.5's tokenizer on JSON/tool-call strings, but the paper does not run any such audit itself.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper touches the vocabulary-size question relevant to this project ("Does a 65K vocabulary handicap tool-call and JSON tokenization against the 128K-vocab models?") only in the abstract/general sense — it cites Tao et al. (2024) that "larger models deserve larger vocabularies" and notes vocabulary expansion of "5–10K tokens" tends to yield better returns than aggressive scaling, but gives no code/JSON-specific fertility numbers, no data at the ~1B scale, and explicitly disclaims empirical grounding: "Our argument centers on reframing tokenization as a modeling decision, but it remains primarily conceptual. We do not provide new empirical results or large-scale ablations" (Limitations).
- No content on quantization, GGUF conversion, or how tokenizer/vocabulary choices interact with low-bit quantization of embedding/output layers — a gap relative to this project's 4-bit GGUF requirement.
- No instruction-like text directed at the reader was found in the paper; it reads as a standard academic survey.
