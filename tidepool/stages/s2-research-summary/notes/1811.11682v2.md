# 1811.11682v2 — Experience Replay for Continual Learning

**Relevance:** medium
Not an LLM/quantization/tool-calling paper, but its core empirical result — mixing replayed old-task data with new-task data in a fixed ratio prevents catastrophic forgetting without hurting new-task learning — directly speaks to the project's open question of keeping general ability from eroding while a narrow (tool-calling) skill is trained up.

## TL;DR

This is a 2019 DeepMind RL paper (NeurIPS) introducing CLEAR, a method that trains an actor-critic agent on a 50-50 mixture of fresh on-policy experience and replayed off-policy experience (plus a behavioral-cloning loss against the historical policy/value outputs) to nearly eliminate catastrophic forgetting across sequentially-trained DMLab/Atari tasks. It's useful here only as a general analogy/data point for "replay old data alongside new data to preserve prior skills," not as a directly applicable method for our LLM/GGUF pipeline.

## Techniques to try

- Rehearsal/data-mixing ratio as a tunable stability-plasticity knob, with an empirically-grounded default: "We believe that 50-50 new-replay represents a good tradeoff, combining significantly reduced catastrophic forgetting ... with no appreciable decrease in performance attained." (Section 4.4) — this generalizes as a prior for our own SFT/DPO data-mix ablation (mix general-capability data with tool-calling data rather than fine-tuning on tool data alone).
- Small buffers are nearly as good as huge ones: "We find that all buffers perform well and conclude that it is possible to learn and reduce catastrophic forgetting even with a replay buffer that is significantly smaller than the total number of experiences." (Section 4.5) — suggests a small held-out slice of general/instruction data mixed into task-specific fine-tuning may suffice; doesn't need to be a large corpus.
- Explicit "cloning" penalty toward the pre-update model's outputs on replayed/retained data (KL on policy distribution + L2 on value function) as a second stability lever beyond data mixing (Section 3, Lpolicy-cloning/Lvalue-cloning) — conceptually similar to KL-to-reference-model regularization already common in DPO/RLHF, worth noting as validated evidence for that style of regularizer.

## Baselines & datasets used

- DMLab task suite (`explore_object_locations_small`, `rooms_collect_good_objects`, `rooms_keys_doors_puzzle`, `natlab_varying_map_randomized`) trained via IMPALA/V-Trace actor-critic.
- Atari suite (`space_invaders`, `krull`, `beam_rider`, `hero`, `star_gunner`, `ms_pacman`) compared against Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and Progress & Compress (P&C), matching the setup and hyperparameters from the P&C paper.
- All results at the scale of a single IMPALA-style actor-critic network (no parameter counts given; not an LLM) — not comparable in scale or modality to our 1.2B-parameter model.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- 100% replay (pure off-policy training, no fresh on-policy data) prevents forgetting but degrades plasticity: "unlike with standard CLEAR, the performance obtained on the probe task ... deteriorates markedly as it appears later in the sequence of tasks" because the new task becomes a shrinking fraction of the replay mix (Section 4.3) — a direct caution that skewing too heavily toward "old/general" data during specialization can suppress acquisition of the new skill.
- The smallest tested buffer (1-in-200 subsampling of experience) showed some renewed catastrophic forgetting, attributed to "over-fitting to the limited examples present in the buffer" (Section 4.5) — a caution that a rehearsal set that's too small/narrow can itself be over-fit.
- The paper explicitly flags a failure mode outside its scope: "if the action space of a task changed during training, fitting to the old policy's action distribution ... could have a deleterious effect on performance" (Section 5) — i.e., replay/cloning-style stability methods can actively fight a genuinely necessary behavior change, not just noise.

## Metric / eval choices

- Distinguishes "interference" (two tasks compatible/incompatible when trained jointly) from "catastrophic forgetting" (performance drop purely from sequential training order) via three matched conditions: separate, simultaneous, and sequential training with total experience held constant (Section 4.1) — a clean experimental design pattern (not a metric) that could translate to isolating whether our tool-calling fine-tuning genuinely damages general benchmarks vs. merely reflects less exposure.
- Uses cumulative/area-under-curve reward as the headline comparison number (Figure 3, Figure 7) rather than only final performance, to capture the cost of transient forgetting during training, not just the end state.

## Open questions for our project

- No content in this paper reads as an instruction directed at the reader; it is a standard NeurIPS paper describing RL experiments.
- Nothing here bears on quantization, GGUF, tool-schema validity, LoRA vs. full fine-tuning, or reward hacking — the paper is entirely in the deep-RL continual-learning setting and offers no directly transferable numbers for BFCLv3, IFEval, or throughput targets.
