# 2412.04787v3 — Direct Quantized Training of Language Models with Stochastic Rounding

**Relevance:** low
Real topic overlap (quantization-aware training and low-bit weight robustness) but the method is for training LLaMA-style models from scratch at 130M–1B scale, not fine-tuning or quantizing an existing pretrained/instruction-tuned checkpoint, and it never touches GGUF, instruction-following, tool calling, or throughput — nothing here is directly actionable for shipping a 4-bit GGUF of LFM2.5-1.2B.

## TL;DR

The paper proposes Direct Quantized Training (DQT): instead of keeping full-precision "shadow" weights and a straight-through estimator (as BitNet does), it applies stochastic rounding directly to the optimizer's updated weights so the model's weights stay at n-bit precision (ternary up to 8-bit) throughout the entire training process, cutting training memory. Experiments are pretraining runs (not fine-tuning) of 130M/320M/1B LLaMA-architecture models on Wikipedia/FineWeb, showing 8-bit DQT roughly matches or beats BitNet b1.58 and is far more robust to BF16/FP8 memory reduction, while ternary (1.58-bit) DQT converges but lags meaningfully behind higher precision.

## Techniques to try

- Stochastic rounding as a drop-in for STE when doing low-bit training: "we directly update the quantized low-precision weights during backpropagation... we use stochastic rounding... to preserve the low-precision format of the weight matrices after backpropagation and minimize the information loss" (Section 1) — only relevant if the project's last-resort "quantization-aware training" step ever needs a from-scratch or heavily-retrained low-bit model; not applicable to PTQ/GGUF conversion of an already fine-tuned checkpoint.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Baselines: reproduced FP32 LLaMA2-architecture models and reproduced BitNet b1.58 (ternary weights, STE-based training) ("The second baseline is the reproduced BitNet b1.58, a well-known QAT method that employs ternary weights at inference but relies on high-precision weights during training." — Section 4.1).
- Datasets: English Wikipedia (20231101.en) for 130M/320M models; FineWeb (10B-token sample) for 1B models; evaluation on WikiText-2 perplexity plus zero-shot WinoGrande, ARC (easy/challenge), PIQA, and SciQ via lm-eval (Section 4.1, Table 1).
- All models are LLaMA2-architecture, trained from random initialization for one epoch — no instruction tuning, no chat/tool-call data, no models above 1B parameters.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Naive quantization without stochastic rounding fails to converge at all in the ternary regime: "we compare 130M ternary DQT with a variant that simply uses absmax quantization on the updated weight matrices... the latter fails to converge, despite operating under the same bit budget" (Section 5.1) — a warning that any custom low-bit training/QAT path needs a mechanism (stochastic rounding or equivalent) to preserve small gradient updates, or it collapses.
- Ternary (1.58-bit) DQT models converge but still show a clear performance gap versus FP32 and versus 8-bit DQT: "although there remains a performance gap compared to higher-precision models, the ternary DQT implementation still demonstrates the ability to converge" (Section 4.2) and bit-width ablation shows "as the number of bits used in DQT increases, the model's performance improves consistently for both 130M and 1B models" (Section 4.4) — i.e., very aggressive quantization (ternary/1.58-bit) measurably hurts quality relative to 8-bit or higher, even before considering instruction-following or structured-output tasks specifically.
- Results and conclusions are drawn only at ≤1B scale with generic LM benchmarks (perplexity, WinoGrande, ARC, PIQA, SciQ) — no instruction-following, tool-use, or structured-output evaluation is reported anywhere in the paper, so it cannot speak to whether low-bit quantization disproportionately harms schema validity or tool calling versus general knowledge.

## Metric / eval choices

- Reports WikiText-2 perplexity plus zero-shot accuracy on WinoGrande/ARC-easy/ARC-challenge/PIQA/SciQ via lm-eval, all at fixed 512 sequence length matching training (Section 4.5, Table 1) — generic knowledge/reasoning benchmarks, not applicable to this project's BFCL/IFEval/structured-output metrics.
- GPU memory usage is measured as a percentage of a single GH200 GPU's memory under their training setup (Section 4.3), not decode throughput or inference latency on any consumer GPU, so it gives no evidence toward the project's 5%-throughput-floor target.

## Open questions for our project

- None of DQT's numbers transfer to a fine-tuning-then-PTQ workflow: the method assumes training from scratch with a custom optimizer (modified AdamW/Adafactor with stochastic rounding) that is not part of standard GGUF/llama.cpp quantization tooling, so it does not directly inform how to hit the ≥97%-retention / ≤5%-throughput-loss targets for the 4-bit GGUF build.
- No instruction-tuned or tool-calling evaluation is present, so the paper cannot answer this project's core open question of whether low-bit quantization damages structured output/instruction-following more than general knowledge — it only shows that lower bit width degrades generic perplexity/QA accuracy, at ≤1B scale.
