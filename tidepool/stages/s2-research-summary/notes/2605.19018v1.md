# 2605.19018v1 — LoRA vs. Full Fine-Tuning: A Theoretical Perspective

**Relevance:** medium
Directly addresses the project's open "LoRA or full fine-tuning at 1.2B?" question with theory plus small-model (0.5B–3B) empirical evidence, but the LLM experiments are simple classification tasks (yes/no, multiple-choice), not structured/tool-call generation, and nothing here touches quantization, throughput, or schema-validity metrics.

## TL;DR

Using a linear-regression model of fine-tuning, the authors show LoRA's main advantage over full fine-tuning (FFT) is variance reduction, not expressivity, and that advantage only materializes when the true task-specific update is effectively low-rank and/or the data is noisy. They confirm the qualitative pattern on Qwen2.5 (0.5B/1.5B/3B) fine-tunes of BoolQ and CommonsenseQA: whether LoRA beats FFT depends on the task's own spectral structure, not on any universal rule.

## Techniques to try

- Sweep LoRA rank rather than assuming a single "safe" rank works across tasks — in their sweep ($r\in\{1,8,32,64,128\}$, scaling fixed at $\alpha=2r$) the best rank was task- and noise-level dependent ("in the weak noise regime, no consistent ordering across ranks is observed... in contrast, in the strong-noise regime, performance decreases monotonically with increasing rank" — Sec. 4).
- If the SFT/preference data is expected to be noisy (e.g. synthetically generated malformed-tool-return pairs, imperfect labels), prefer lower LoRA rank or LoRA itself over FFT: "As the noise increases, FFT degrades rapidly and becomes much less robust than low-rank LoRA" (Sec. 4).
- To decide LoRA-vs-FFT for a specific fine-tune before committing compute, the paper's method is to first train a strong reference model (FFT + multi-seed weight averaging) and inspect the per-layer singular-value decay of the resulting weight delta — a fast-decaying spectrum predicts LoRA will match or beat FFT; a heavy tail predicts no LoRA advantage ("For CommonsenseQA, the reference update exhibits a rapidly decaying spectrum... Correspondingly, LoRA significantly outperforms FFT... In contrast, for BoolQ, the spectral tail is heavier... LoRA provides no significant advantage over FFT" — Sec. 4, App. VI.11).

## Baselines & datasets used

- Qwen2.5 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, fine-tuned on BoolQ and CommonsenseQA, Alpaca-style instruction format, 3 epochs, effective batch size 32, bf16, single A100 per job (App. VI.1–VI.3, VI.10).
- LoRA config used as baseline recipe: adapters on "all-linear targets—every linear projection in every transformer block," $\alpha = 2r$ fixed scale, PEFT defaults ($A\sim\mathcal N(0,1/r)$, $B=0$ init), lr $2\mathrm{e}{-4}$ for $r\le64$ and $1\mathrm{e}{-4}$ for $r=128$ (App. VI.5).
- Full-FT baseline used both AdamW (lr $5\mathrm{e}{-5}$ everywhere, best across all cells) and SGD with momentum (lr strongly cell-dependent, tuned per cell) plus multi-seed weight averaging for SGD (App. VI.4, VI.6).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- No consistent rank ordering under low label noise: intermediate rank was optimal in some cells with no clean trend (Sec. 4) — a rank sweep is needed per task rather than reusing one rank.
- LoRA's advantage is not guaranteed: "when the spectrum of $\Delta^{\star}$ is flat, i.e., the singular values are approximately uniform, the bias can be significant... LoRA would perform poorly" (Sec. 3.1) — confirmed empirically on BoolQ, where "LoRA provides no significant advantage over FFT" (Sec. 4).
- FFT's excess risk blows up near the interpolation threshold ($n \approx d_x$), i.e. small/borderline dataset sizes relative to the number of trainable parameters are a real failure mode independent of LoRA vs FFT choice (Sec. 3.1, Prop. 3).
- Related-work summary is itself a pitfall list worth carrying over: Shuttleworth et al. found LoRA training creates "intruder dimensions" (new high-ranking singular vectors) making its weights spectrally unlike FFT's; Biderman et al. found LoRA "often underperforms FFT on tasks such as coding, math, and instruction fine-tuning, but it suffers less from catastrophic forgetting"; Ivison et al. found QLoRA "falls short of FFT on long-form generation tasks" (Sec. 5) — long-form/structured generation is closer to this project's tool-call/JSON output task than the paper's own BoolQ/CSQA experiments.

## Metric / eval choices

- Core metric is expected excess risk (population risk minus the risk of the true generator), not training loss — a generalization-focused framing, evaluated zero-shot via lm-evaluation-harness for the LLM experiments (Sec. 2.3, App. VI.1).
- Diagnostic device worth reusing: per-layer normalized cumulative spectral energy of the fine-tuning weight delta, $s(r)=\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2(W)/\|W\|_F^2$, used to predict whether a given fine-tune will favor LoRA or FFT (Sec. 4).

## Open questions for our project

- The paper's own limitations note that the linear-regression theory doesn't provably transfer to LLMs ("showing that LLMs also exhibit an NTK regime is beyond the scope of this paper... the qualitative empirical observations from our LLM experiments are only suggestive" — Sec. 6), so treat its guidance as a heuristic, not a guarantee, for a 1.2B hybrid-architecture model doing structured tool-call generation rather than classification.
- Would the "train a reference model, inspect the spectral decay of its weight delta" method be worth doing cheaply on a subset of our tool-calling data before committing to the LoRA-first rung of the candidate ladder, or is it not worth the extra training run given the ladder already tries LoRA first and escalates on failure?
- No content in this paper reads as an instruction to the reader; it is a standard theory/experiments paper and was treated purely as descriptive source material.
