# 2602.22592v1 — pQuant: Towards Effective Low-Bit Language Models via Decoupled Linear Quantization-Aware Training

**Relevance:** low
Real topical overlap (quantization sensitivity, mixed-precision branches) but the method operates in a completely different regime from this project — sub-2-bit QAT-from-scratch on custom transformer architectures with bespoke CPU kernels, not post-hoc 4-bit quantization of an already fine-tuned 1.2B model into a standard GGUF for llama.cpp-style deployment.

## TL;DR

pQuant trains transformer LLMs from scratch at ~1.3-bit average precision by splitting each FFN linear layer into a dominant 1-bit branch plus a small learnable 8-bit "high-precision" branch (treated like a routed MoE expert) that the model learns to allocate its most quantization-sensitive weights into. It beats BitNet/BitNet1.58 baselines on perplexity and zero-shot commonsense QA at matched scale and data budget, but everything is evaluated on knowledge/commonsense tasks, not structured output or instruction following, and requires training the whole model from random initialization rather than quantizing an existing checkpoint.

## Techniques to try

- The core "protect the sensitive subset in higher precision, leave the rest at 1-bit" idea is conceptually similar to per-tensor/per-layer precision choices already available in GGUF quantization (e.g. keeping certain projections at higher bit-width) — worth remembering as a rationale if the eventual 4-bit GGUF plan needs mixed-precision tensors to hit the ≥97%/93% floor, even though pQuant's specific mechanism (learnable feature-scaling router, MoE-style top-1 gating) is not something applicable post-hoc: ("we introduce *feature scaling*, where the outputs of the two FFN branches are modulated by learnable scalars $\alpha$ and $\beta$" — §3.2)
- Their finding on where sensitivity concentrates could inform which sublayers to prioritize protecting if the team ever does QAT-style fine-tuning at 4-bit: ("FFN layers are known to concentrate a larger fraction of sensitive parameters that are critical to model performance" — §3.1)

## Baselines & datasets used

- BitNet (1-bit), BitNet1.58 (2-bit), and FP16 LLaMA-2 trained from scratch at matched scale/data budget (100B tokens) on C4, Wikipedia, and ArXiv; also compared against PTQ1.61, OmniQuant, and OneBit at their own published scales.
- Evaluation is zero-shot accuracy on ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, BoolQ, PIQA, Winogrande, OpenBookQA, HellaSwag, plus WikiText-2 perplexity — no instruction-following, tool-calling, or structured-output benchmark anywhere in the paper.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Naively fixing which weight positions get high precision does not work in a from-scratch setting: ("there exists no consistent spatial pattern in the weight matrix that reliably indicates where sensitive parameters reside... Predefining fixed positions for high-precision weights thus diminishes the potential benefit" — §3.2)
- A simpler "just keep more parameters in high precision" baseline underperforms the learned/routed approach even with a larger high-precision budget: ("we explored directly keeping 8% of parameters in high precision from the start of BitNet training. This approach yields significantly worse performance than pQuant, despite using more high-precision parameters (8% vs. pQuant's 5%)" — §4.6)
- 1-bit training from scratch is reported as unstable at larger batch sizes and prone to gradient explosion requiring checkpoint rollbacks, a pitfall for anyone considering training (not just post-hoc quantizing) at extremely low bit-width: ("BitNet frequently suffers from gradient explosion during training, often requiring checkpoint reloading and restarts" — Appendix G / Fig. 10 caption)
- Group-wise quantization gets better accuracy than the paper's scheme but is flagged as impractical for deployment: ("Group-wise quantization (with block size 64) achieves better accuracy but requires storing one 16-bit scaling factor per 64 1-bit weights, which incurs substantial metadata overhead and is unfriendly to hardware" — §4.6)
- Explicitly out of scope for this project: the method needs training from random initialization (not post-training quantization of an existing checkpoint), custom 1-bit/8-bit mixed kernels via a CPU library called T-MAC, and was trained on "16 NVIDIA A100-80G GPUs" for what the paper itself calls a training-cost limitation confining experiments to ≤2.6B scale.

## Metric / eval choices

- Sensitivity is measured with a GPTQ/SPQR-style perturbation metric — squared output distortion from zeroing a weight under the inverse-Hessian correction — used only for architecture-design diagnostics, not as a training or eval signal transferable to this project's tool-call/schema metrics.
- All downstream evaluation is commonsense-QA accuracy and perplexity; there is no evaluation of schema validity, tool-call correctness, or malformed-input flagging, so this paper cannot speak to the project's BFCLv3 or flag-rate targets.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper never tests whether extreme quantization disproportionately harms structured-output/instruction-following versus general knowledge — the exact question the brief asks about — so it neither supports nor refutes that hypothesis for this project.
- All results are for models trained from scratch at 1–1.35 bit average precision; it's unclear whether any of the sensitivity/decoupling insight generalizes to 4-bit post-training quantization of an already-instruction-tuned 1.2B checkpoint, which is the actual task here.
- No sign of embedded instructions directed at a reader in the paper text; it reads as a standard research paper throughout.
