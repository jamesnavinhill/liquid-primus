# 2604.18419v4 — Knowing When to Quit: A Principled Framework for Dynamic Abstention in LLM Reasoning

**Relevance:** medium
Introduces a lightweight, theoretically-grounded "abstain when the model probably won't succeed" mechanism (probe on hidden states, no extra decoding cost) that is conceptually close to our "flag rather than assert" sub-criterion, but it is validated only on long chain-of-thought math/toxicity tasks at 7B/3.8B scale, not on single-turn structured-output/tool-call flagging at 1.2B.

## TL;DR

The paper casts mid-generation abstention as an MDP: at every token position, estimate the value function (probability the final answer will be correct) and stop generating if it drops below a tunable reward `r_⊥`. They prove this "dynamic value-thresholding" rule dominates no-abstention and fixed-position abstention baselines, and show a cheap two-layer MLP probe trained with binary cross-entropy on hidden states recovers the value function well enough in practice, beating self-assessment prompting and LoRA-trained abstain tokens on math reasoning (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Phi-3-mini-4k-Instruct) and toxicity avoidance (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct).

## Techniques to try

- Train a small MLP probe on final-layer hidden states with binary cross-entropy to predict eventual outcome correctness, and threshold it to decide "continue vs. flag/abstain" — this is architecturally cheap ("compared to the 7B and 4B-parameter models tested in Section 6, the two-layer MLP adds roughly $10^{4}$ parameters, making probe inference and training overhead negligible") and could be adapted as a secondary classifier over the base model's hidden states to decide whether to flag a malformed tool return rather than assert, instead of relying only on prompting or an explicit fine-tuned "abstain token."
- Calibrate the abstention rate via an $\alpha$-quantile threshold on a small held-out set rather than trying to hit an absolute reward value directly: "the threshold $T_{\alpha}$ is the $\alpha$-quantile of estimated values on a small held-out set, and transfers to new data with mean absolute error below 1.2 percentage points."

## Baselines & datasets used

- Models: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Phi-3-mini-4k-Instruct (both larger than our 1.2B target).
- Datasets: GSM8K and OlympiadBench (chain-of-thought math), plus RealToxicityPrompts (Gehman et al., 2020) for a toxicity-avoidance generalization check.
- Baselines compared: Constant Step Probe (BCE-trained MLP at a fixed token position), Self-Assessment (prompting "Can you correctly answer this question?" and reading yes/no logits), LoRA Abstention (LoRA-finetuned explicit abstain/do-not-abstain tokens), and no-abstention base policy.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- The two baseline families closest to what a team might try first for our "flag rather than assert" sub-criterion both underperformed: "self-assessment and LoRA abstention show limited discrimination ability, often performing near or below the no-abstention baseline," and "observing $k$ tokens of partial generation does not substantially help the self-assessment and LoRA methods: the fixed-position variants plateau near the no-abstention accuracy regardless of $\alpha$." This is a direct warning that naive self-assessment prompting or a LoRA-trained explicit flag token may not reliably discriminate good vs. bad outcomes without a dedicated probe/training signal.
- Fixed-position abstention (choosing one token position $k$ to make the call) is inherently arbitrary: "Both choices are arbitrary: the optimal position varies across datasets, models, and desired abstention rates, and there is no principled data-independent criterion for selecting $k$."
- The method's benefit depends on the reward/probe being informative; the paper flags "the method is limited when hidden states do not encode sufficient information about eventual correctness, or when optimization fails to extract this signal," though they judge this not severe in their tests.
- Calibration between an estimated threshold and the "true" effective abstention reward $r_{\bot}$ is imperfect and needs a correction step (isotonic regression) — a subtlety worth remembering if we ever try to set our flag-rate/false-flag-rate targets from a raw learned score rather than a calibrated one.

## Metric / eval choices

- Selective accuracy (accuracy among non-abstained samples) plotted against abstention rate — analogous in spirit to our flag-rate / false-flag-rate pair, though defined differently (this paper measures accuracy conditional on not abstaining, we care about detecting bad tool returns specifically).
- Abstention precision, $\mathbb{P}(\text{incorrect}\mid\text{abstained})$ (Appendix J), used to confirm abstentions are selectively targeted — structurally close to our false-flag-rate idea, but computed as the complementary probability.
- Reward objective $J_\beta$ under zero KL regularization, used to empirically validate the dominance theorems.
- Token savings relative to input-only baselines, as an efficiency metric for early termination.

## Open questions for our project

- Their probe-based abstention targets *mid-generation CoT termination* for math/toxicity tasks; our sub-criterion is a single-turn decision (flag a malformed tool return vs. continue) — it's unclear the value-function/probe framing transfers cleanly without an analogous notion of "trajectory value" for a one-shot structured-output decision.
- All experiments are at 7B/3.8B scale; no evidence on whether a 1.2B model's hidden states carry enough signal for a comparably reliable probe, which matters given our much smaller hidden size (2048).
- The paper never touches quantization, structured/JSON output, or tool-calling, so it gives no direct evidence on the project's core BFCL/schema-validity questions — its relevance is limited to the general idea of a lightweight learned "should I trust this continuation" signal.
- No instruction-like content directed at the reader was found in the paper; it is a standard research paper with abstract, theory, experiments, and discussion sections.
