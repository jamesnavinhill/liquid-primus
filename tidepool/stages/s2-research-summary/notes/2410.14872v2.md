# 2410.14872v2 — How to Evaluate Reward Models for RLHF

**Relevance:** low
Real topical overlap (DPO training procedure, verifiable-correctness reward signals, reward-model over-optimization) but the paper targets reward-model benchmarking for general chat RLHF on 8B+ models via Chatbot Arena, not tool-calling/structured-output, quantization, or small on-device models — nothing here settles a decision for this project.

## TL;DR

The paper (PPE) builds a reward-model benchmark meant to predict post-RLHF downstream human-preference performance, using both crowdsourced Chatbot Arena preference pairs and verifiable-correctness pairs (math, code, MMLU-Pro, GPQA, IFEval), then validates it by actually running DPO on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with nine different reward models and measuring real Arena scores. It's a reward-model evaluation methodology paper, not a post-training or tool-calling paper, so it doesn't touch this project's core levers (quantization, GGUF, structured output, tool schemas).

## Techniques to try

- None directly transferable. The closest adjacent idea: use "verifiable correctness" datasets (code unit tests, regex matching) as a preference/reward signal rather than LLM-judge scores — conceptually similar to this project's planned GRPO reward of "schema validity and execution correctness," though PPE applies it to benchmark reward models, not to train directly ("we can score the correctness (a binary label) of each response according to the original static benchmark's verification function (e.g. code unit tests or Regex matching)" — Section 5).

## Baselines & datasets used

- DPO training baseline: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct fine-tuned via DPO using 8,000 prompts (7,000 from Arena preference votes + 500 MMLU-Pro + 500 MATH), each with 16 sampled responses at randomized temperature, "chosen" = max reward-model score, "rejected" = a randomly-ranked lower response (Section 6.1). This is an 8B model, above this project's 1.2B target, and uses off-policy DPO with externally-scored reward models — the opposite of the "no model-scored stored reward may be reused as a training signal" constraint here.
- Correctness benchmarks used for verifiable preference labels: MMLU-Pro, MATH, GPQA, MBPP-Plus, IFEval — several of these (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, IFEval) overlap directly with this project's own guardrail metrics, but only as external benchmarks, not as training techniques.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Reward-model benchmark scores can be actively misleading at the high end: "as reward models have improved, we now see a negative correlation between RewardBench evaluation score on top models and downstream RLHF performance" (Section 2.2) — a caution that a proxy metric (e.g. schema-validity reward) can decouple from real downstream quality as it's optimized against.
- Aggregate/coarse metrics (Spearman/Kendall rank correlation, row-wise Pearson) "have nearly zero correlation with the final Arena Score achieved by the post-DPO models" (Section 7) — only fine-grained, per-example accuracy correlated well (Pearson ~0.77 overall). Relevant caution for this project: don't trust coarse aggregate eval numbers as a proxy for whether a specific training signal (e.g., a schema-validity reward) will actually improve the deployed behavior — check per-example accuracy/robustness, not just averages.
- Over-optimization ("reward hacking") is visible directly in their data: "Performance on MBPP Plus benchmark showing many reward models over-optimizing" (Figure 2 caption) as K increases in best-of-K sampling — a concrete illustration of a reward proxy being gamed as exploration increases, relevant to this project's own concern about GRPO reward hacking when the reward is a schema check.
- Lower-bound/worst-case performance is a better predictor of real downstream quality than average or best-case: "we observe that measuring the lower bound score may correlate more to downstream RLHF performance than the average score or upper bound score... Any domain weakness in a reward model can be exploited by the LLM during training" (Section 7) — supports designing held-out probes (like this project's malformed-tool-return probe) around worst-case/robustness rather than average-case behavior.

## Metric / eval choices

- Uses pairwise accuracy, Spearman/Kendall rank correlation, Pearson correlation of win-rate matrices, ROC-AUC on binary correctness, and best-of-K curves (max achieved, error vs. ground truth, end score) as candidate reward-model metrics, then empirically finds accuracy and ROC-AUC correlate best with real downstream preference (Section 4.2, 5.2, 7). Not directly reusable for this project's BFCL/IFEval/schema-validity metrics, but the meta-lesson — validate that an offline metric actually predicts the real deployed outcome before trusting it — applies to this project's own untested claim that first-attempt schema validity or flag-rate targets are realistic.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper offers no guidance on small (~1B-class) models, quantization effects, tool/function-calling, or structured-output reliability — none of the project's core open questions (65K vocab tokenization penalty, 4-bit quantization damage to structured output, LoRA vs. full fine-tuning at 1.2B, flag-rather-than-assert training signal) are addressed here.
- Given the project's constraint that "no model-scored stored reward may be reused as a training signal," this paper's whole premise (train a reward model, or use one to generate DPO pairs) sits outside the project's allowed design space — worth confirming that using verifiable/programmatic correctness signals only (not LLM-scored rewards) remains consistent with that constraint when the ladder reaches GRPO.
