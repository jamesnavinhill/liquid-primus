# 2602.12247v2 — ExtractBench: A Benchmark and Evaluation Methodology for Complex Structured Extraction

**Relevance:** medium
Its evaluation methodology (missing/null/hallucination distinction, per-field metrics) and its negative result on constrained decoding are useful context for our structured-output eval design, but the paper studies frontier-scale models (GPT-5, Gemini-3, Claude 4.5) doing PDF-to-JSON extraction with huge schemas, not tool-calling/agent-turn output at 1.2B scale, and proposes no training method.

## TL;DR

ExtractBench is a PDF-to-JSON extraction benchmark (35 docs, 5 domains, 12,867 fields) plus a schema-driven evaluation framework that assigns each field its own scoring metric (exact/fuzzy/semantic/tolerance) and separately tracks omission vs. hallucination vs. structural failure. Frontier models fail badly as schema breadth grows (0% valid JSON on a 369-field schema), and — notably for us — provider-native constrained decoding / structured-output modes *reduced* both validity and accuracy versus plain prompting on complex schemas.

## Techniques to try

- Schema-driven per-field evaluation configs (exact match for IDs, tolerance for numbers, LLM-judged semantic equivalence for free text, LLM-based array alignment for object arrays) could be a template for designing our own tool-call/structured-output scoring beyond a single pass/fail parse check ("the schema should define not just *what* to extract, but *how* to evaluate each field" — §3.1).
- Explicit three-way value-state handling (present / null / MISSING) to separate omission from hallucination errors is directly analogous to our "flag rather than assert" problem: "When gold has a value but predicted is MISSING, the error is *omission*... When gold is null but predicted has a value, the error is *hallucination*" (§3.3) — worth adapting for scoring the malformed-tool-return probe (distinguish "failed to flag" from "false-flagged a good return").

## Baselines & datasets used

- Six frontier models compared: Gemini 3 Pro/Flash, GPT-5/5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5/Opus 4.5 — all far larger than our 1.2B target, so absolute pass-rate numbers don't transfer, only the qualitative failure patterns.
- Benchmark domains: SEC 10-K/Q filings (369 schema keys), credit agreements (13 keys, 100–250 pages), research papers (16 keys, 100+ item citation arrays), resumes (31 keys), sports results (12 keys, depth 6) — none of these resemble our tool-call/agent-turn prediction unit (system prompt + tool schemas + tool results → one structured call).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Constrained decoding made things worse, not better, on complex schemas: "structured outputs *reduce* both validity and accuracy relative to prompt-based extraction: overall validity dropped from 51% (107/210) to 37% (77/210), and the best-model pass rate fell from 6.9% to 5.5%" (§4.3, GPT-5/5.2, Gemini-3, Claude 4.5). This is direct outside evidence supporting our project's constraint of measuring **first-attempt schema validity with no constrained decoding or grammar enforcement** — the underlying capability, not a decoding crutch, is what should be trained.
- Constrained-decoding APIs also outright reject schemas: "the resume schema – which produced 62% validity in prompt mode (26/42) – was rejected outright by all providers in structured mode (0/42)" (§4.3) — a hard failure mode with no analogue in prompt-based generation.
- Accuracy degrades even on *valid* constrained output: "GPT-5's pass rate on credit agreements fell from 86.9% to 70.0%, suggesting that the constrained decoding overhead ... may compete with the model's capacity to attend to document content" (§4.3) — a caution if we ever consider grammar-constrained decoding as a deployment-time fallback.
- Schema breadth, not depth or document length, drives failure: "0% valid output on a 369-field financial reporting schema across all tested frontier models" (Abstract) while a depth-6 schema (sports) still hit 90% validity — output volume/complexity is the binding constraint, not context length. Our schemas (tool args, tool-call structures) are far smaller than 369 fields, so this failure mode is unlikely to bite us directly, but it's a reminder that schema complexity, not just prompt length, matters for reliability at any scale.
- Common failure modes were mundane formatting bugs, not semantic ones: trailing commas before closing braces (Claude/Gemini, concentrated on the hardest schema) and truncated JSON — suggesting some SFT/DPO training signal on strict-JSON well-formedness could pay off cheaply, independent of semantic correctness.

## Metric / eval choices

- "Valid JSON" (parseable + schema-conforming) vs. "Pass rate" (field-level correctness against schema-declared metric) are reported as two separate numbers, with invalid extractions counted as zero in the pass-rate denominator — a useful template for reporting our own first-attempt-validity metric alongside content correctness rather than conflating them.
- LLM-as-judge is used for semantic string equivalence and for array alignment (matching predicted vs. gold array items), with a stated pass threshold (0.7) and a Levenshtein threshold (0.8) for fuzzy string matching — concrete threshold values if we need an LLM-judge component, though our project's constraint against reusing a "model-scored stored reward... as a training signal" means this can inform *evaluation* design but not directly the RL reward.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper's array-level semantic alignment (LLM judge matches predicted/gold array items, then scores per-field) is a heavier-weight eval mechanism than we likely need for tool-call argument scoring; unclear whether a lighter deterministic scheme suffices for our schemas, which are presumably much shallower and narrower than 369-field financial schemas.
- No results at anything near 1.2B scale, and no training method (SFT/DPO/GRPO/quantization) is discussed at all — this paper answers none of our candidate-ladder or quantization questions directly.
