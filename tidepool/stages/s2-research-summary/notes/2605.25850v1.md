# 2605.25850v1 — TIAR: Trajectory-Informed Advantage Reweighting for LLM Abstention Learning

**Relevance:** high
Directly addresses the project's open question of "which training signal instils flag-rather-than-assert" by giving a concrete, ablated GRPO reward design for the correct/wrong/abstain trade-off, at a size (8B) small enough to inform (not just gesture at) the project's GRPO stage.

## TL;DR

TIAR is a GRPO reward-shaping trick for teaching an LLM to say "I don't know" instead of guessing: rather than a fixed ternary reward (correct=+1, wrong=-1, abstain=0), it uses the model's own empirical correctness rate across the G sampled rollouts for a query to dynamically re-price abstention, applied as a post-normalization advantage adjustment (not a reward-function change) to avoid distorting GRPO's group statistics. On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B it beats a static ternary baseline (TruthRL) on abstention F1 in most categories while preserving accuracy, evaluated on AbstentionBench.

## Techniques to try

- Reward the abstain trajectory dynamically from the group's own attempted-answer accuracy rather than a fixed constant: "The ternary reward thus implicitly sets a fixed abstention threshold at $\hat{p}^{*}=0.5$, regardless of the actual difficulty distribution encountered during training." — §3.2. Directly relevant to designing the GRPO schema-validity/execution-correctness reward for flagging malformed tool returns: a fixed "flag reward = 0" may be miscalibrated the same way.
- Apply the abstention-reward correction as a **post-hoc advantage adjustment**, not a reward-function edit, to avoid corrupting GRPO's group mean/std for the other trajectory types: "TIAR ... applies the derived insight as a post-hoc advantage adjustment rather than a reward modification. The key idea is to preserve the standard GRPO normalization for correct and incorrect trajectories while separately adjusting the abstention advantage based on the group's empirical difficulty." — §3.4. This is a generic pattern for the project's own GRPO reward (schema validity + execution correctness): don't let a "flag" outcome's reward distort the advantage baseline for well-formed-output trajectories.
- Use the spread of G sampled rollouts per prompt as a built-in confidence/difficulty signal instead of a separate calibration head or verifier: "Leveraging GRPO's multiple trajectories as a natural abstention signal, this method uses a reward signal to explore knowledge boundaries and encourage consistency." — Abstract.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Base models: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B (§4.1) — above the project's 1.2B target scale but within the brief's "at or below 8B" preference; no result shown at ~1B.
- GRPO training data: "the TruthRL-CRAG dataset ... which contains 656 training samples derived from the CRAG benchmark" (§4.1) — small, single-turn QA dataset; not a tool-calling/structured-output dataset.
- Baselines compared: R-Tuning (SFT with relabeled "I don't know"), RFT (rejection fine-tuning on correct-only responses), DPO (preference pairs with IDK preferred over wrong answers), and TruthRL (GRPO, static ternary reward) — §4.1. This ladder (SFT → RFT/DPO → GRPO) parallels the project's own candidate ladder.
- Eval benchmark: AbstentionBench, "a comprehensive benchmark containing 20 datasets across 31 subsets spanning six abstention scenarios" (§4.2) — a QA/knowledge-boundary benchmark, not a tool-call/JSON-schema benchmark, so scores don't transfer numerically to BFCL-style targets.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Naively substituting the dynamic reward into the standard GRPO reward function (rather than decoupling it as a post-hoc advantage adjustment) measurably hurts accuracy: "a coupled implementation degrades accuracy by $-0.76\%$ compared to the ternary baseline, despite improving abstention F1 by $+0.15\%$." — §3.3, Table 1. Direct warning for the project: if the schema-validity/execution-correctness reward is folded into the same GRPO reward as the primary task reward, it can distort the advantage baseline for the primary (non-flagging) trajectories.
- The reweighting strength is not "more is better" in a monotonic way and small settings can backfire: "Setting $\lambda=0.3$ causes a collapse in both metrics, demonstrating that a small inversion weight destabilizes training without providing sufficient signal." — §5.2, Table 3.
- Method is bottlenecked by reward-signal quality: "our methodology heavily relies on an external LLM judge ... to accurately assign ternary rewards during the RL training phase ... Misclassifications by the judge directly translate into noisy advantage signals during GRPO normalization." — Limitations. Relevant given the project's own ban on reusing a model-scored *stored* reward — this paper's judge is used online per-rollout (not stored/reused), which may or may not satisfy that constraint; worth resolving explicitly (see below).
- GRPO's per-query rollout cost is called out as a scaling limiter: "the structural requirement of generating multiple trajectories ($G=8$ in our experiments) per query during GRPO training incurs a non-trivial computational overhead compared to supervised fine-tuning or DPO ... which limits its immediate scalability for researchers with restricted GPU budgets." — Limitations. Compute used: 16 A100 40GB GPUs, ~6.7 hours for 20 steps, 107.2 GPU-hours total for one 8B model, single seed only (no repeated runs) — a real resource data point, though at a different model scale than 1.2B.
- Explicitly flagged as untested beyond single-turn QA: "our experimental evaluation is strictly constrained to single-turn question-answering scenarios ... Real-world abstention is rarely binary; it frequently occurs in complex, multi-turn dialogues where an agent might ask clarifying questions, request specific context, or provide a partial answer rather than outright abstaining." — Limitations. The project's "flag" behavior for malformed tool returns is closer to this multi-turn/agentic case than to the paper's tested setting.

## Metric / eval choices

- Primary metric is abstention F1 (harmonic mean of abstention precision/recall) with accuracy reported as an independent, separately-tracked capability axis, explicitly not conflated: "Since AbstentionBench showed no direct relationship between abstention F1 and accuracy, our goal is to improve abstention while preserving accuracy as an independent capability indicator." — §1. This mirrors the project's own paired-metric design (flag rate + false-flag rate, tracked so neither is gameable alone).
- Judge reliability is reported and non-perfect: "This judge was validated against human annotation at 82.3% accuracy by the AbstentionBench authors." — §4.2. A concrete ceiling/reference point for how much noise an LLM-judge-based flag/correctness label can carry.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper's "abstain vs. attempt" decision is a knowledge-boundary judgment; the project's "flag vs. assert" decision is a malformed-tool-output judgment. Does the empirical-correctness-rate proxy ($\hat{p}$ from G rollouts) even make sense for the latter, where "correctness" of a tool-return-handling trajectory may be closer to a deterministic parse/execution check than a fuzzy knowledge question?
- Would the decoupled-advantage-adjustment idea need modification for a two-outcome (well-formed/malformed) setup rather than the paper's three-outcome (correct/wrong/abstain) setup?
- The paper relies on an external LLM judge to assign reward during RL — does an *online, non-stored* per-rollout judge call count as the "model-scored stored reward" the project's standing constraints prohibit reusing as a training signal, or is it out of scope of that constraint? This needs an explicit decision before adopting anything reward-judge-shaped from this paper.
- No result at anywhere near 1.2B parameters or with a 65K-vocab / hybrid-architecture model — unclear whether a much smaller model has stable-enough rollout diversity (G=8) for the $\hat{p}$ proxy to be meaningful, or whether it's noisier and needs a larger G or different smoothing.
