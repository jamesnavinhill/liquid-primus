# 2601.03525v3 — Beyond Binary: Turning Partial Success into Dense Verifiable Rewards for Reinforcement Learning in Code Generation

**Relevance:** medium
Directly relevant to the GRPO-with-verifiable-rewards rung of the candidate ladder and to the "how is reward hacking avoided" question, but everything is measured on code generation with abundant per-problem unit tests (4B/8B Qwen3), not on tool-call/schema correctness at 1.2B, so the technique needs re-derivation rather than adoption as-is.

## TL;DR

VeRPO replaces binary GRPO outcome rewards (pass/fail on the full test suite) with a reward built from per-test-case pass/fail outcomes, but shows that naively densifying with the raw pass rate hurts performance because a "cardinality bias" lets the large mass of already-easy tests dominate the group-mean baseline and drown out progress on hard ("frontier") tests. Their fix — density-calibrated, difficulty-weighted per-test rewards fused with a global binary outcome reward — beats plain GRPO and an external-reward-model baseline on Qwen3-8B/4B code generation benchmarks, with claimed negligible compute overhead.

## Techniques to try

- Combine a dense "local" verifiable reward with a binary "global" outcome reward rather than either alone, via a single unified advantage: "$A(\tau_{i},t)=A^{traj}(\tau_{i})+\beta\cdot A^{turn}(\tau_{i},t)$" (Eq. 14, Sec 4.2) — directly analogous to combining schema-validity correctness with execution correctness in our GRPO stage.
- If we ever decompose "schema validity" or "execution correctness" into multiple sub-checks (e.g., per-field JSON Schema conformance, per-argument correctness, per-step tool-call correctness), watch for the cardinality bias they describe and consider density-normalizing sub-check weights rather than just difficulty-weighting them: "redundant, easy tests dominate the optimization baseline by their sheer count, causing policy optimization to disproportionately favor gains from easy-test successes rather than progress on frontier tests" (Abstract).
- Track the "degenerate group ratio" (fraction of rollout groups with identical/zero-advantage rewards) as a diagnostic for whether a verifiable reward is too sparse to produce gradient signal in GRPO — useful even if we don't adopt VeRPO itself, since our schema-validity/execution-correctness rewards risk the same sparsity: "outcome-driven GRPO maintains a high degenerate group ratio, typically between 60% and 70%" (Sec 5.2.3).
- Fixed-normalization advantage ($F_{\text{norm}}=1$, an unbiased leave-one-out estimator) is used instead of GRPO's default std-normalization, and ablates better: "replacing the fixed normalization with standard-deviation normalization consistently underperforms VeRPO" (Sec 5.2.2) — a candidate tweak if we implement GRPO ourselves.

## Baselines & datasets used

- Training data: 7.4K verified problems from TACO (subset curated by Luo et al. 2025/DeepCoder), mean 104±70 test cases per problem.
- Eval benchmarks: HumanEval / HumanEval-Plus, BigCodeBench (Full/Hard), LiveCodeBench V6, Codeforces (via CodeElo). All code generation with executable test suites, not tool-calling or structured JSON output.
- Backbones: Qwen3-8B (main results), Qwen3-4B (appendix robustness check) — both above our 1.2B target scale but the smaller (4B) result is the closer proxy.
- Baselines compared: vanilla GRPO with binary outcome reward, and AceCoder (external 7B reward-model-based dense reward, AceCoderRM-7B).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Naively densifying the reward is actively harmful, not just unhelpful: "a denser reward is not necessarily better... the naive pass-rate reward is both dense and verifiable, it can still degrade final performance compared to binary outcome-driven rewards" (Sec 1). In their ablation, raw pass-rate reward alone underperforms plain GRPO on BigCodeBench-Hard, LiveCodeBench, and Codeforces (Table 3).
- Difficulty-weighting the reward without correcting for test-case density is still insufficient: "difficulty weighting alone does not address the cardinality bias identified in Section 4.1; density calibration is needed to make partial success a reliable learning signal" (Sec 5.2.2).
- Relying on an external, model-scored reward for dense supervision caused outright training collapse in the multi-turn setting: "AceCoder fails to complete stable MT training due to optimization collapse... suggesting the instability risk of relying on an external black-box reward model for iterative optimization" (Sec 5.2.1), attributed to the RM's inability to "exhaustively cover the infinite semantic space of complex programs," yielding "misaligned signals for out-of-distribution responses" (Appendix F.1). This is independent evidence supporting our project's standing constraint that no model-scored stored reward may be reused as a training signal — here a learned RM destabilized RL even when it wasn't literally reused, just used online.

## Metric / eval choices

- Uses the unbiased pass@k estimator from Chen et al. 2021 (HumanEval paper) with n=8 samples per problem to estimate pass@1, rather than raw one-shot accuracy — worth reusing if we ever report pass@k-style estimates for tool-call correctness sampled multiple times.
- "Degenerate group ratio" (fraction of GRPO rollout groups with zero relative advantage) is used as a signal-efficiency diagnostic distinct from final task accuracy — a useful metric to log during our own GRPO runs regardless of whether we adopt VeRPO.

## Open questions for our project

- All results are on code generation with dozens to hundreds of test cases per problem (mean 104 test cases in their training set); our tool-call/schema-validity rewards will likely have far fewer sub-checks per example (a handful of schema fields or one tool-call correctness check), so it's unclear the density-calibration machinery (Gaussian KDE over per-test pass rates) transfers to such a low-cardinality setting — this needs to be worked out rather than assumed.
- No results below Qwen3-4B; nothing at 1.2B scale, and no discussion of quantization, instruction-following retention, or general-knowledge erosion, which are central to our project's guardrail metrics.
- The paper is entirely about single/multi-turn code solution generation, not about tool-call structured output or "flag vs. assert" behavior on malformed inputs, so it offers no direct evidence on our core sub-criteria (flag rate / false-flag rate on malformed tool returns).
