# 2309.05444v1 — Pushing Mixture of Experts to the Limit: Extremely Parameter Efficient MoE for Instruction Tuning

**Relevance:** medium
Quantifies the LoRA-vs-full-fine-tuning gap and offers a mixture-of-LoRA-experts technique to close it, but everything is measured on T5 encoder-decoder classification-style zero-shot tasks, not on a decoder-only model or on tool-calling/structured-output/quantization objectives.

## TL;DR

The paper replaces standard MoE feed-forward experts with tiny PEFT adapters (IA3 vectors or LoRA matrices) as the "experts," giving a Mixture of Vectors (MoV) or Mixture of LoRA (MoLORA) layer that updates well under 1% of a T5 model's parameters yet approaches or matches full fine-tuning on unseen (zero-shot) tasks. It also runs ablations on routing strategy, number of experts, and hyperparameter sensitivity that are informative if this project ever considers a mixture-of-adapters approach instead of a single LoRA.

## Techniques to try

- Mixture of lightweight PEFT experts (MoV/MoLORA) to close the gap between plain LoRA and full fine-tuning cheaply: "MoV using 30 experts achieves a 14.57% performance improvement compared to its dense counterpart (IA)3" and "MoV and MoLORA both with 10 experts, are on par with full fine-tuning" while MoV-10 updates only 0.32% of a 3B model's parameters (Section 4, Table 1).
- Prefer soft merging (weighted average of all experts) over discrete top-k routing at this parameter scale: "we observe that soft merging significantly outperforms discrete routing in the MoV-10 setting... discrete routing with top-k experts, where k is 1 and 2, the MoE achieves an average median accuracy of 54.92 and 57.45 respectively. In contrast, using the soft merging approach, where all experts are activated, we observe an accuracy of 59.93." (Section 4.3)
- Route on token-level hidden representations rather than a precomputed sentence/instruction embedding: "We find that the token routing exhibits superior performance with 3.03%, 8.86%, and 0.94% improvement for 770M, 3B, and 11B base model sizes respectively" over Sentence-T5-embedding-based routing (Section 4.4).
- Use small batch size and a modest learning rate (3e-4 in their setup) when training adapter/expert layers, since aggressive optimization destabilizes the mixture (see Pitfalls).

## Baselines & datasets used

- Base models: T5 v1.1+LM adaptation at 770M, 3B, and 11B parameters (Raffel et al., 2020).
- Training data: P3 (Public Pool of Prompts), "a collection of 62 datasets covering a wide variety of tasks" (Sanh et al., 2022).
- Zero-shot evaluation on 8 held-out (unseen during training) datasets: ANLI, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, and 5 SuperGLUE tasks (CB, RTE, WSC, WiC, COPA), scored as median accuracy across prompt templates per dataset, then averaged.
- Baselines compared: full fine-tuning (their own re-tuned T0 replication, which "performs significantly higher than the original T0"), plain (IA)3, and plain LoRA (rank 4).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Plain LoRA (rank 4, 0.3% of params) clearly underperforms full fine-tuning at 3B: 57.51 vs 60.06 average median accuracy (Table 1) — a concrete, at-scale number for how much capacity a single low-rank adapter leaves on the table versus full FT, relevant to the "LoRA or full fine-tuning at 1.2B?" open question (directionally; measured on a different architecture/task family).
- Larger batch sizes destabilize MoE-adapter training and collapse it toward a single active expert: "the larger the batch size, the more likely our MoEs to collapse to a single expert... by experimenting with a batch size of 2048 and evaluating every 5K steps up to 20K, we observed that the performance of our parameter-efficient MoEs deteriorates after 5K steps, converging to performance levels akin to their dense counterparts" (Section 4.6).
- Adding a load-balancing auxiliary loss (standard in sparse MoE) made things worse in this extreme-PEFT regime: "we find that the top-k selection of k = 2 with load balancing loss leads to a further decrease in performance 1.5 average median score" (Section 4.3).
- The authors explicitly flag their own scope limit: "A primary constraint of our experimental framework is its focus on text-to-text models, such as T5, without extending the evaluation to decoder-only such as GPT style models... our assessment is exclusively within the context of fine-tuning [not pre-training]" (Limitations) — i.e. none of this was validated on a decoder-only architecture like the project's target model.

## Metric / eval choices

- Zero-shot generalization to explicitly unseen task types (not held-out examples of trained tasks), following the T0 protocol — median accuracy per dataset across multiple prompt templates, with mean also reported in the appendix.
- No BFCL-style structured-output, schema-validity, tool-call, or quantization metrics anywhere in the paper — the eval surface is standard NLU/commonsense classification benchmarks.

## Open questions for our project

- All results are on T5 (encoder-decoder, up to 11B) with classification/multiple-choice-style zero-shot tasks; it's unclear whether the MoV/MoLORA soft-merging benefit would transfer to a 1.2B hybrid conv+GQA decoder-only model or to JSON/tool-call generation objectives — nothing here directly grounds BFCL, schema-validity, or flag-rate targets.
- The paper doesn't touch quantization, throughput, or GGUF export at all, so it can't inform the "does low-bit quantization damage structured output more than knowledge" question.
- If a mixture-of-LoRA-experts approach were ever considered as a step beyond plain LoRA on the candidate ladder, its batch-size/load-balancing sensitivities (see Pitfalls) would need to be re-validated at 1.2B scale before relying on them.
- No embedded instructions directed at the reader were found in the paper text; it reads as a standard research paper throughout.
