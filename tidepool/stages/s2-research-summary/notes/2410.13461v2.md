# 2410.13461v2 — Progressive Mixed-Precision Decoding for Efficient LLM Inference

**Relevance:** medium
Gives concrete small-model (1.4B–7B) evidence that low-bit weight quantization hurts instruction-following and reasoning before it hurts general fluency/knowledge, but the actual method (dynamic, runtime precision-switching with multiple resident weight copies) doesn't transfer to a static 4-bit GGUF single-precision deployment target.

## TL;DR

The paper shows that quantizing LLM weights uniformly across the whole generation is wasteful: the prefill phase and the earliest decoded tokens are much more sensitive to precision loss than tokens generated later, so they propose keeping prefill/early-decode at higher bitwidth and progressively dropping to lower bitwidth deeper into the sequence (PMPD), using either an offline "static" per-task schedule or a small learned scheduler conditioned on the prefill KV cache. It's evaluated on Vicuna-7B, MobileLLaMA-1.4B, Phi-1.5, StableLM Zephyr-3B (plus Llama3-8B/Longchat-13B in the appendix) on summarization/translation/open-ended QA, not on structured output or tool-calling tasks.

## Techniques to try

- Phase-aware precision allocation (high-precision prefill KV cache extraction, lower-precision decode) as a cheap first check when diagnosing whether 4-bit degradation on our project is concentrated in prompt understanding vs. generation: "our inference scheme dictates that a high-precision model is used during the prefill phase which in turn enables the use of lower-precision weights in the memory-bound decoding phase... Since the prefill phase is compute-bound... using a high-precision model during prefill introduces negligible latency overhead." (Section 4.1) — not directly applicable since our deployment ships one fixed 4-bit weight set, but worth knowing if we ever consider a mixed-precision inference server instead of a single GGUF.
- If a calibration/validation set is available for a target task, an offline "static" schedule (precedence-constrained, minimizing high-precision token count subject to a quality floor) beat a learned scheduler in most of their tasks: "the Static scheduler achieves a slightly higher bitwidth reduction (0.66 vs. 0.63 bits) and frequently outperforms Learned, showing more stable performance with less variance." (Section 5.1)

## Baselines & datasets used

- Baselines: single-precision PTQ (via Any-Precision LLM's nested quantization, Park et al. 2024) and Dense-and-Sparse Quantization (DNS / SqueezeLLM, Kim et al. 2024) matched to PMPD's average bitwidth.
- Datasets/tasks: CNN/DM (summarization), Dialogsum (dialogue summarization), IWSLT French-English (translation), MT-Bench (open-ended QA), scored with Rouge-L, BERTScore, BLEU/SacreBLEU — none are structured-output, tool-call, or JSON-schema tasks.
- Models at or below 8B: Vicuna-7B, MobileLLaMA-1.4B, Phi-1.5 (1.3B), StableLM Zephyr-3B; also tests generalization to Llama3-8B and Longchat-13B (Appendix A.3) but those are above our size class.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- At 2-bit, instruction-following collapses even though text stays fluent: the 2-bit Vicuna-7B "struggles with instruction following and often outputs incomplete tokens or repeated prompts" and a 3-bit prefill fixes this ("Rouge-L on CNN/DM improves by an average of 4.3 points, BERTScore on Dialogsum increases by 3.6, and BLEU improves by 9.45" from phase-aware allocation alone, Section 5.3) — direct evidence that quantization damage concentrates in instruction-following/reasoning before general coherence, relevant to our project's open question about whether low-bit quantization disproportionately harms structured output vs. knowledge.
- On the translation task (IWSLT), the static scheduler "experiences a moderate performance drop of up to 2.3%" even after tuning — i.e. this technique is not lossless everywhere, and translation/precision-sensitive tasks fared worst among those tested.
- A qualitative failure case at 3/4-bit mixed precision on Vicuna-7B: a simple modular-arithmetic word problem was answered with a fluent but wrong non-sequitur procedure (Appendix A.8) — a concrete example of reasoning failure surviving under a scheme the paper otherwise calls "lossless."
- The frequent per-token precision-alternation schedule "also proves to work well" algorithmically but is explicitly rejected for deployment: "frequent precision switching introduces hardware overhead, both in terms of weights loading time and additional memory traffic, so this schedule is not considered practical for real deployment" (Section 3.2) — a caution against any scheme requiring multiple resident weight copies or per-step precision switches on our single 8GB GPU target.
- GPU utilization was suboptimal even with speedups achieved: "CPU-side processes for GPU kernel launching become a bottleneck, resulting in suboptimal GPU utilization" (Section 5.2), left as future work (CUDA Graphs) rather than solved.

## Metric / eval choices

- Quality is measured against the same model's own fp16/higher-precision output (Rouge-L/BERTScore similarity to a reference generation) rather than against task ground truth in most tables — i.e. many of their "lossless" claims are about fidelity-to-full-precision-output, not task accuracy per se. Worth noting since our project's guardrail metrics (IFEval, MMLU-Pro, GPQA, BFCL) are all judged against ground truth/execution, a stricter bar than output-similarity.
- They calibrate the "lowest lossless precision" per phase using perplexity on C4, a generic corpus, not a task-specific or instruction-following benchmark — a possible pitfall if applied naively to our project, where perplexity-based precision selection could look fine while structured-output validity or tool-call correctness is quietly broken (the exact class of gap the reader brief's guardrail metrics are meant to catch).

## Open questions for our project

- Their method requires shipping/holding multiple quantized weight variants simultaneously (via Any-Precision LLM's nested representation) so precision can be switched mid-generation — this conflicts with our single fixed 4-bit GGUF artifact requirement and 1.5GB disk budget; not clear PMPD's approach reduces to anything usable in a standard llama.cpp/GGUF export pipeline.
- None of the evaluated tasks involve tool calls, JSON schema, or structured output, so this paper cannot directly ground the BFCL/schema-validity targets; it only supports the softer claim that instruction-following degrades faster than fluency under quantization, which is suggestive but not a substitute for a structured-output-specific quantization study.
- All target models are standard dense Transformers; none use LFM2.5's hybrid short-convolution + GQA architecture, so it's unknown whether the prefill/decode error-resilience asymmetry the paper reports (attributed partly to attention "sink" tokens) even holds for a mostly-convolutional model.
