# 2510.10390v1 — RefusalBench: Generative Evaluation of Selective Refusal in Grounded Language Models

**Relevance:** high
Directly grounds the project's flag-rate/false-flag-rate metric design and its "0.80/0.10 realistic or optimistic" question with a closely analogous refusal-evaluation study, and gives a concrete training-signal comparison (DPO > SFT for refusal behavior) relevant to the candidate ladder — even though the task domain is RAG QA text refusal rather than malformed tool-call detection.

## TL;DR

RefusalBench is a generative benchmark methodology that perturbs answerable QA instances into six categories of "informational uncertainty" (ambiguity, contradiction, missing info, false premise, granularity mismatch, epistemic mismatch) at three intensities, then measures whether 30+ models correctly refuse. Even frontier models are bad at this: refusal accuracy is below 50% on multi-document RAG and models trade off false refusals against missed refusals rather than solving both. The paper also shows refusal is "a trainable, alignment-sensitive capability" that DPO improves more than SFT and that scale alone does not reliably fix.

## Techniques to try

- Use a paired metric design instead of a single flag-rate number, to prevent gaming one side: "False Refusal Rate (FRR): The proportion of answerable instances incorrectly refused, measuring over-cautious behavior." / "Missed Refusal Rate (MRR): The proportion of unanswerable instances incorrectly answered, measuring potentially harmful over-confidence." (Appendix D, "Core Behavioral Metrics") — directly mirrors this project's flag-rate/false-flag-rate pair.
- Split "should we flag" from "did we get the reason right" into two separately-scored sub-skills, as the paper does for refusal: "Refusal Detection F1-Score... measuring the model's ability to identify when refusal is appropriate" and "Category Accuracy: Given correct refusal decisions, this metric evaluates the accuracy of predicted refusal reasons" combined into a "Hierarchical Refusal Score: The product of Detection F1-Score and Category Accuracy" (Appendix D). Could inform scoring the malformed-tool-return probe as detection-of-malformed vs. correct-diagnosis-of-what's-wrong.
- DPO as a training signal for refusal-type behavior outperformed SFT in this paper's controlled comparison: "Direct DPO significantly impacts both answering and refusal capabilities compared to SFT. For refusal accuracy, DPO provides consistent improvements across all scales, with the largest gain at 7B (3.4x improvement)." (§4.2, "Effect of Alignment Methods") — measured on OLMo-2 SFT vs DPO variants, 1B–32B, on RAG QA refusal, not on tool-call malformed returns.

## Baselines & datasets used

- RefusalBench-NQ: derived from NaturalQuestions via KILT ground-truth passages, 1,600 perturbed instances, single-document.
- RefusalBench-GaRAGe: derived from the GaRAGe dataset, 1,506 instances, multi-document RAG, CC-BY-NC-4.0 licensed.
- Models evaluated for scale/alignment effects: Qwen 1.5 (0.5B–72B), OLMo-2 DPO/SFT (1B–32B), Llama 3.1 (8B, 70B) — all well below the project's 1.2B target is only matched by the smallest Qwen/OLMo/Llama points, so scaling trends here are only loosely transferable.
- Frontier models compared: GPT-4o/4.1/o4-mini, Claude-4-Opus/Sonnet/3.5-Sonnet, DeepSeek-R1, Amazon Nova-Pro/Premier — none at the project's scale, useful only as an upper bound on what "good" refusal behavior looks like.

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Flag-rate and false-flag-rate genuinely trade off against each other rather than both improving together: "the strong negative correlation (r = -0.78 on NQ) between false and missed refusals forces models to choose between being overly cautious or overly permissive" (§4.2, RQ2) — a concrete warning that the project's "flag rate ≥ 0.80 with false-flag rate ≤ 0.10" pair may be fighting itself.
- Scale alone does not fix refusal/flagging behavior: "Qwen remains persistently low across all sizes (<17%)" for refusal accuracy despite scaling from 0.5B to 72B, even while its answer accuracy jumps sharply between 4B and 7B (§4.2, "Effect of Scale") — suggests a 1.2B model should not be expected to "just flag correctly" without targeted training.
- Extended inference-time reasoning gave negligible benefit for this behavior: "inference-time reasoning has a negligible effect on Claude-4-Sonnet's performance. Extending its reasoning trace with up to 4096 thinking tokens yields less than a 1pp improvement in refusal accuracy" (§4.2, "Effect of Reasoning Length") — a soft signal against expecting GRPO-style extra reasoning tokens alone to fix flagging, though this is inference-time reasoning, not RL training.
- Models default to a catch-all category under uncertainty rather than diagnosing correctly: "models default to REFUSE_INFO_MISSING as a catch-all category. On RefusalBench-NQ, this category receives 25% of all predictions" (§4.2, "Systematic Misclassification of Refusal Types") — a failure mode to check for in the malformed-tool-return probe (e.g., always flagging "missing field" regardless of actual defect).
- Severe overconfidence even when wrong: "more than 73% of predictions occur at maximum confidence despite 40-69% accuracy" (§4.2, "Severe Miscalibration but Better on Refusals") — relevant risk for a model that must flag rather than assert on garbage tool output.
- Self-evaluation/LLM-judge bias is large and inconsistent: models rate their own generations more favorably than peers (91.0% self vs 82.1% cross-eval pass rate on NQ), with per-model bias ranging from strongly negative to +25.8pp, and inter-verifier agreement as low as κ=0.061 (§4.2, RQ1) — a caution against relying on a single model-as-judge (or the model itself) to validate the malformed-tool-return probe or any generated eval data.

## Metric / eval choices

- Calibrated Refusal Score (CRS): "computed as the arithmetic mean of Answer Accuracy and Refusal Accuracy" (Appendix D) — a simple composite balancing "don't over-flag" against "don't under-flag."
- Expected Calibration Error (ECE) reported separately for answer vs. refusal predictions, since the paper finds "they are better calibrated on refusals (ECE 0.226-0.519) than answers (ECE 0.406-0.580)" (§4.2) — worth considering as a secondary diagnostic alongside flag-rate/false-flag-rate.
- Generative/perturbation-based evaluation (programmatically transforming answerable instances into flawed ones at controlled intensity) is offered as a way to avoid benchmark contamination/memorization; the project's ~300 malformed tool-return probe is conceptually similar (synthetically corrupting well-formed tool returns) and could borrow the "intensity levels" idea (LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH severity of the injected defect) to stress-test where the flag-rate/false-flag-rate boundary sits.

## Open questions for our project

- The taxonomy here (ambiguity, contradiction, missing info, false premise, granularity mismatch, epistemic mismatch) is built for natural-language QA context defects, not malformed structured tool returns (bad JSON, wrong types, schema violations, execution errors, timeouts). Does an analogous taxonomy of "malformed tool-return" defect types already exist for the project's probe, or is this paper's category system worth adapting?
- The DPO > SFT result for refusal-type behavior is measured on OLMo-2 (1B-32B) on RAG QA, not on a 1.2B tool-calling model with this project's guardrail metrics — directionally suggestive for the candidate ladder's DPO step, but not direct evidence at this scale/task.
- No instruction-like text directed at the reader was found in the paper; it is a standard research paper with no embedded directives.
