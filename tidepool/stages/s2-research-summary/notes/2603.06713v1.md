# 2603.06713v1 — Scaling Agentic Capabilities, Not Context: Efficient Reinforcement Finetuning for Large Toolspaces

**Relevance:** medium
Directly relevant to the project's GRPO/RFT questions for tool-calling SLMs, but its central mechanism (LLM-judge rubric rewards) is the exact "model-scored stored reward" this project's standing constraints prohibit, and its smallest model (4B) and task shape (long-horizon, multi-server MCP orchestration) sit well above the project's single-turn 1.2B prediction unit.

## TL;DR

ATLAS trains Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-4B with GRPO to operate over large MCP toolspaces by (1) loading server/tool schemas incrementally instead of upfront and (2) orchestrating tool calls via a persistent Python interpreter instead of turn-by-turn JSON, then reinforcement-finetunes both with rewards from an LLM judge scoring fixed, task-specific rubrics rather than a single scalar. The paper's main claim for this project's purposes: task-level rubric rewards judged by an SLM (Qwen3-30B) beat both generic scalar LLM-judge rewards and rubric rewards judged by GPT-4o, and RFT contributes far more of the final score than execution-structure changes alone.

## Techniques to try

- Mask loss over tool-output/observation tokens during RL so gradient updates only touch model-generated tokens, not injected tool-response text — a cheap, judge-independent trick applicable to any tool-use RL setup: ("During optimization, gradients are masked over tool-output tokens so that learning applies only to model-generated content." — Section 3.1)
- Decompose "success" into fixed, weighted sub-criteria per task (task fulfillment, tool appropriateness, tool grounding, parameter accuracy) instead of one holistic pass/fail judgment, to reduce reward variance across rollouts of the same task in GRPO's group-relative advantage estimation — the general idea of category-weighted, task-fixed rubrics could be reimplemented with verifiable (non-LLM) checks for this project's schema-validity/execution-correctness reward rather than an LLM judge: ("By holding rubrics constant across all trajectories for a given task, this approach enforces consistent relative judgments, reduces variance in reward signals" — Section 3.1)

## Baselines & datasets used

- MCPBench (Wang et al. 2025): 28 live MCP servers, 257 tools, synthetic multi-server tasks generated with o4-mini and filtered by solvability/utility judges; final curated training set is 304 tasks (filtered further by requiring Kimi-K2 Thinking to score task fulfillment ≥4/10, i.e. solvable by a strong model).
- Models: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (both 32K context) as the SLMs being trained; Kimi-K2 Thinking (1T params, 80K context) as frontier baseline with eager tool loading.
- Training infra: "We use the verl library as the reinforcement learning framework and extend it to support MCP tool calling" (Section 4.4), on 8×NVIDIA B200 GPUs; GRPO with train batch 16, PPO mini-batch 4, rollout n=4, temperature 1.0, LR 1e-6, KL coeff 0.001, max 20 tool calls, max tool response length 4000 tokens (Appendix B).
- Judges: rubrics generated once per task offline by GPT-5; training-time reward judge is SLM Qwen3-30B-Instruct or, in ablations, GPT-4o; final held-out evaluation judge is o4-mini in all conditions (kept fixed to isolate training effects from evaluation bias).

## Pitfalls / negative results

- Structural changes tuned for small models can hurt a frontier model trained differently: "Kimi does not benefit from ITL (TF drops to 3.62, Row 3), as it is not trained to reason over JSON-restricted tool views" — a caution that context-scoping/tool-loading tricks are not universally beneficial and interact with what the base model was originally trained/tuned to expect.
- Generic (non-rubric) LLM-as-judge rewards are explicitly flagged as noisy for RL: "variability in the judge's internal reasoning and strictness can lead to inconsistent relative rankings, injecting noise into the learning signal and undermining stable policy optimization" (Section 3, "Limitations of LLM-as-Judge") — relevant supporting evidence for why this project's constraint against model-scored training rewards is reasonable, not just a preference.
- Execution-structure changes meant to reduce context can increase token cost: Programmatic Tool Calling raised average tokens (e.g. Qwen3-4B ITL 9,045 → ITL+PTC 13,462 tokens) despite reducing turn count and eventually improving task fulfillment once combined with RL — a caution that "fewer round-trips" and "less context" are not the same axis.
- Structure alone (no learning) gives only modest-to-moderate gains and does not close the gap to a frontier model; the paper states "Structured execution alone is insufficient for small models" and that gains from ITL alone were only "~10%" versus "~25%" from PTC alone, with the largest gains (35–65%) coming only once RFT is applied on top.
- Rubric-based RL reward is explicitly not yet extended to the programmatic (code) execution mode: "we do not yet apply rubric-based rewards to PTC, due to the challenge of defining concrete rubrics over executable code" — i.e. their best-performing configuration (ITL+PTC+RFT) still uses a generic scalar reward, not the rubric reward that is the paper's main proposed innovation.

## Metric / eval choices

- Primary metric is Task Fulfillment (TF) on a 0–10 scale, judged by o4-mini using the MCPBench evaluation prompt, applied identically across all training conditions to keep evaluation bias out of the comparison: "Following MCPBench, we use o4-mini as the LLM judge for its robustness in validation" (Section 4.3).
- Four judged sub-axes reported in detailed tables (Appendix C): Task Fulfillment, Grounding, Tool Appropriateness, Parameter Accuracy — plus Avg Turns and Avg Tokens as efficiency metrics, tracked alongside quality so that context/turn savings are not reported in isolation from task success.
- Held-out generalization is split into in-distribution (104 unseen MCPBench tasks, same 28 servers) and out-of-distribution (100 tasks over 11 new servers) — a two-tier generalization check (task-level vs. server/tool-level shift) that is a reasonable template for testing whether this project's tool-calling gains transfer beyond the fine-tuning distribution's specific tool schemas.

## Open questions for our project

- The paper's central reward mechanism — an LLM judge (Qwen3-30B or GPT-4o) scoring rubric criteria and that score being used directly as the GRPO reward — is precisely the kind of "model-scored stored reward" this project's standing constraints prohibit as a training signal. None of the paper's rubric-reward machinery can be adopted as-is; only the judge-independent pieces (tool-output token masking, the idea of decomposing reward into fixed weighted sub-criteria if those criteria can be made verifiable) look portable.
- All experiments are at 4B/7B/30B scale on long-horizon, multi-server, multi-turn MCP orchestration tasks — a different prediction unit than this project's single-turn structured-output/tool-call generation at 1.2B. It's unclear whether the reported RFT gains (which come largely from credit assignment over long, compositional trajectories) say anything about single-turn schema validity or malformed-tool-return flagging, which is this project's actual sub-criterion of interest.
- No LoRA vs. full-finetuning comparison is reported (full RFT is used throughout), so this paper does not inform the project's own LoRA-vs-full-FT question at 1.2B.
- No quantization or on-device deployment discussion at all — irrelevant to the project's 4-bit GGUF requirement.
